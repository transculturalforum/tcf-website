Zoroastrianism

One of the world's oldest continuously practiced monotheistic religions, originating in ancient Persia and grounded in the cosmic struggle between truth and falsehood.

Introduction

Zoroastrianism is one of the world's oldest continuously practiced religions. It was founded in ancient Iran by the prophet Zarathushtra, known in Greek as Zoroaster.

Zoroastrians believe in one supreme, wise, and good God, Ahura Mazda, the creator of all that is good. They teach that the world is the scene of a struggle between good and evil, truth and the lie, and that every person must freely choose to stand on the side of good.

Zoroastrianism was the main religion of several great Persian empires for more than a thousand years. Many scholars believe that its ideas about one God, angels, judgment, heaven and hell, resurrection, and a final renewal of the world influenced Judaism, Christianity, and Islam, though the extent of this influence is debated.

Zoroastrians understand their faith not simply as belief but as a way of life based on three principles:

"Good thoughts, good words, good deeds." (Humata, Hukhta, Hvarshta)

These three words are the best-known summary of the faith.

Zoroastrianism at a Glance

Name

Zoroastrianism

A follower is called a Zoroastrian. The religion is also known as Mazdayasna, "the worship of Mazda," and its followers as Mazdayasnians.

Zoroastrians in India are known as Parsis ("Persians"), and those who remained in Iran are often called Iranis or Iranian Zoroastrians.

Approximate Number of Adherents

The world's Zoroastrian population is small: estimates generally range from about 100,000 to 200,000 people.

India's 2011 census counted about 57,000 Parsis. Iran has an estimated 15,000 to 25,000 Zoroastrians. The rest live in diaspora communities.

The community has been declining in many places because of low birth rates, emigration, and marriage outside the faith.

Some estimates are higher when they include people in Iran, Central Asia, and Kurdish regions who identify with Zoroastrian heritage.

Where Zoroastrianism Is Practiced

Zoroastrians live mainly in:

  • India, especially Mumbai and Gujarat
  • Iran, especially Tehran, Yazd, and Kerman
  • the United States
  • Canada
  • the United Kingdom
  • Australia
  • Pakistan, especially Karachi
  • the Gulf states
  • New Zealand

Avestan is the sacred language of Zoroastrian scripture. Middle Persian (Pahlavi) was used for later religious texts. Parsis speak Gujarati and English, and Iranian Zoroastrians speak Persian and Dari.

Major Branches and Traditions

Zoroastrianism does not have major denominations, but there are important communities and differences:

Parsis

Descendants of Zoroastrians who migrated from Iran to India, traditionally in the 8th to 10th centuries CE.

Iranian Zoroastrians

Descendants of those who remained in Iran.

Calendar Differences

There are three calendars in use: Shahenshahi, Kadmi, and Fasli. This means some festivals are celebrated on different dates by different groups.

Traditional and Reformist Views

Communities differ on matters such as accepting converts, interfaith marriage, and funeral practices.

Important Zoroastrian Terminology

  • Ahura Mazda: "Wise Lord"; God
  • Zarathushtra (Zoroaster): The prophet of the faith
  • Avesta: The Zoroastrian scriptures
  • Gathas: Hymns composed by Zarathushtra
  • Asha: Truth, righteousness, cosmic order
  • Druj: The lie, falsehood, disorder
  • Spenta Mainyu: The Holy or Bounteous Spirit
  • Angra Mainyu (Ahriman): The destructive spirit
  • Amesha Spentas: Six "Bounteous Immortals," divine qualities
  • Yazatas: "Ones worthy of worship"; divine beings
  • Fravashi: The guardian spirit of each person
  • Atash: Sacred fire
  • Atash Behram: The highest grade of sacred fire
  • Agiary / Dar-e Mehr: Fire temple
  • Navjote / Sedreh-pushi: Initiation ceremony
  • Sudreh: Sacred undershirt
  • Kusti: Sacred cord worn around the waist
  • Chinvat Bridge: The bridge of judgment after death
  • Frashokereti: The final renewal of the world
  • Saoshyant: The future savior
  • Mobed / Dastur: Priest / high priest
  • Nowruz: The New Year

Basic Beliefs

Core Zoroastrian beliefs include:

  1. one supreme God, Ahura Mazda
  2. Zarathushtra as God's prophet
  3. the cosmic struggle between good (asha) and evil (druj)
  4. human free will to choose good
  5. judgment of each soul after death
  6. the final defeat of evil and renewal of the world

The ethical heart of the faith is the threefold path of good thoughts, good words, and good deeds.

Zoroastrians also honor the Amesha Spentas, six divine qualities or beings created by Ahura Mazda:

  1. Vohu Manah — Good Mind
  2. Asha Vahishta — Best Truth
  3. Khshathra Vairya — Desirable Dominion
  4. Spenta Armaiti — Holy Devotion
  5. Haurvatat — Wholeness
  6. Ameretat — Immortality

Each is also linked with a part of creation, such as animals, fire, metals, earth, water, and plants.

Major Zoroastrian Holidays

Nowruz

The New Year, celebrated at the spring equinox, around March 20 or 21. It celebrates renewal and the triumph of light. Nowruz is now celebrated by many peoples of Iran, Central Asia, and beyond.

It is a time of:

  • prayer
  • visiting family and friends
  • new clothes
  • special foods
  • setting a festive table with symbolic items
  • charity

Gahambars

Six seasonal festivals that honor the stages of creation. Communities share meals, and rich and poor eat together.

Muktad (Farvardigan)

The last ten days of the year, when the souls of the departed are remembered.

Other Observances

  • Khordad Sal: the birthday of Zarathushtra
  • Zartosht no Diso: the anniversary of Zarathushtra's death
  • Sadeh: a midwinter fire festival in Iran
  • Mehregan and Tirgan: ancient Iranian festivals

Core Beliefs

God

Zoroastrians believe in one supreme God, Ahura Mazda, the "Wise Lord."

Ahura Mazda is described as:

  • the creator of all good things
  • wise
  • just
  • good
  • the source of truth and light
  • a friend to humanity

Ahura Mazda does not create evil. Evil comes from Angra Mainyu, the destructive spirit, who opposes the good creation.

Zoroastrianism rejects:

  • worship of evil powers
  • the idea that God is the source of suffering
  • the idea that people are puppets of fate
  • falsehood

Zarathushtra

Zarathushtra is the prophet of the faith. Zoroastrians believe Ahura Mazda revealed the truth to him in a series of visions.

Scholars disagree about when he lived. Many place him between about 1500 and 1000 BCE, based on the language of the Gathas, while others suggest a later date around the 7th or 6th centuries BCE. He likely lived in eastern Iran or Central Asia.

According to tradition, he was a priest who received his first vision at age 30, faced strong opposition, and finally found support from King Vishtaspa.

He is honored as a prophet, not as God.

Revelation

Zoroastrians believe Zarathushtra received direct revelation from Ahura Mazda. His own words are preserved in the Gathas, 17 hymns in the Old Avestan language.

The Avesta

The Avesta is the sacred scripture of Zoroastrianism. Much of it was lost over the centuries, especially during invasions. What survives includes:

  • the Yasna, the main liturgy, which contains the Gathas
  • the Visperad, additions to the Yasna
  • the Vendidad (Videvdad), laws of purity and against evil
  • the Yashts, hymns to divine beings
  • the Khordeh Avesta, a book of daily prayers

It contains:

  • hymns of praise
  • teachings on good and evil
  • prayers
  • laws of purity
  • stories of creation
  • descriptions of the end of time

Later Texts

Many Zoroastrian teachings were set down in Middle Persian (Pahlavi) texts from the 9th and 10th centuries CE, including:

  • the Bundahishn, on creation
  • the Denkard, an encyclopedia of Zoroastrian knowledge
  • the Arda Viraf Namag, a vision of heaven and hell

Divine and Spiritual Beings

Zoroastrians honor:

  • the Amesha Spentas, the six "Bounteous Immortals"
  • the Yazatas, divine beings such as Mithra (covenant and truth), Anahita (waters), and Sraosha (obedience and prayer)
  • the Fravashis, guardian spirits of the righteous

Angra Mainyu, also called Ahriman, leads the forces of evil, along with demons (daevas).

Human Nature

Zoroastrianism teaches that humans are created good by Ahura Mazda.

Every person has free will and must choose between good and evil. The Gathas describe this choice as a choice between two primal spirits.

Zoroastrianism does not teach inherited sin. Each person is judged by their own thoughts, words, and deeds.

Sin

Sin is anything that supports the lie (druj) and harms the good creation.

Sin includes:

  • falsehood
  • injustice
  • cruelty
  • pollution of the elements
  • breaking promises
  • harming the good creation

Repentance

Zoroastrianism teaches that wrongdoing can be overcome by repentance and good deeds.

A person who has done wrong is encouraged to:

  1. recognize the wrong
  2. confess it
  3. sincerely regret it
  4. pray for forgiveness, using the Patet prayers of repentance
  5. make amends with good actions

Prayer and Worship

Zoroastrians traditionally pray five times a day, during five periods called gahs.

Common prayers include:

  • the Ashem Vohu, praising truth
  • the Ahuna Vairya (Yatha Ahu Vairyo), the most sacred prayer
  • the Kusti prayers, said while untying and retying the sacred cord

Zoroastrians often pray facing a source of light, such as the sun, a lamp, or fire.

Fire temples house sacred fires. The highest grade, the Atash Behram, may be kept burning continuously for centuries. Only Zoroastrians traditionally enter the inner areas of fire temples in India.

Zoroastrians do not worship fire itself. Fire is a symbol of God's light, purity, and truth.

Charity

Charity is a strong value in Zoroastrian life.

Charity may involve:

  • helping the poor
  • supporting education
  • building hospitals
  • community housing
  • supporting priests and fire temples
  • public service

Parsi philanthropists built many hospitals, schools, and institutions in India.

Fasting

Zoroastrianism generally does not encourage fasting. It values health, joy, and active life. Fasting and extreme self-denial are seen as weakening a person's ability to do good.

Some Zoroastrians avoid meat on certain days of the month dedicated to animals.

Life

Zoroastrianism regards life as good and as a gift of Ahura Mazda.

Human beings are expected to:

  • think, speak, and act well
  • tell the truth
  • work hard
  • marry and raise families
  • care for the elements: fire, water, earth, and air
  • help the poor
  • enjoy life with gratitude

Zoroastrianism rejects withdrawal from the world. It encourages active participation in improving the world.

Death

Death is seen as the work of evil. A dead body is considered impure, so special rites protect the living and the elements from it.

Afterlife

Zoroastrianism teaches:

  1. the soul stays near the body for three days after death
  2. on the fourth dawn it crosses the Chinvat Bridge
  3. the soul is judged
  4. it goes to the House of Song (heaven), the House of the Lie (hell), or an intermediate place
  5. at the end of time, resurrection and the final renewal

Heaven

Heaven is called the House of Song (Garo Demana) or the Best Existence. It is a place of light, joy, and nearness to Ahura Mazda.

Hell

Hell is called the House of the Lie (Druj Demana). It is a place of darkness and suffering.

In Zoroastrian teaching, hell is not eternal. At the end of time, all souls will be purified.

Judgment

After death, each soul's thoughts, words, and deeds are weighed. The soul then crosses the Chinvat Bridge.

Tradition describes the bridge as becoming wide for the righteous and narrow like a blade for the wicked. The righteous soul is met by a beautiful maiden representing its own conscience (daena), and the wicked by a hideous figure.

The Final Renewal

Zoroastrianism teaches that at the end of time, a savior called the Saoshyant will come. The dead will be resurrected, evil will be defeated, and the world will be made perfect again. This renewal is called Frashokereti.

Free Will

Free will is central to Zoroastrianism. Every person freely chooses whether to support good or evil, and each choice matters for the outcome of the cosmic struggle.

Morality

Zoroastrian ethics are summarized in good thoughts, good words, and good deeds.

Among the repeatedly emphasized virtues are:

  • truthfulness
  • justice
  • honesty
  • charity
  • hard work
  • keeping promises
  • loyalty
  • care for the environment
  • respect for parents
  • cleanliness and purity

The Ashem Vohu prayer teaches:

"Righteousness is the best good. Happiness comes to the one who is righteous for the sake of the best righteousness."

Family

Family is central to Zoroastrian life.

Zoroastrianism places particular importance on:

  • parents
  • children
  • spouses
  • elders
  • ancestors

Marriage

Marriage is highly valued and seen as a religious duty. A Parsi wedding (lagan) includes prayers, the blessing of the couple by priests, and the throwing of rice.

Whether children of mixed marriages, especially children of Parsi women who marry outside the faith, are accepted as Zoroastrian is a major debate in the community.

Community

Zoroastrian community life centers around:

  • fire temples
  • community halls
  • charitable trusts
  • the anjuman (local community association)
  • the panchayat (community council)

Treatment of Others

Zoroastrian ethics call for truth and justice toward all.

Cyrus the Great, a ruler of the Persian empire who followed Iranian religious traditions, is remembered for allowing conquered peoples to practice their own religions and for allowing the Jews to return to Jerusalem.

Scriptures and Teachings

The Gathas

The Gathas are the most sacred Zoroastrian texts because they are believed to be the words of Zarathushtra himself.

Their major themes include:

  • Ahura Mazda
  • truth and the lie
  • free choice
  • the good mind
  • devotion
  • justice
  • the final judgment

The Avesta

The wider Avesta includes prayers, hymns, laws, and rituals.

The Vendidad answers practical questions such as:

  • How should purity be kept?
  • How should the dead be treated?
  • How should fire, water, and earth be protected?
  • How should one fight evil?

Zoroastrian Scholarship

Zoroastrian priests preserved the Avesta orally for many centuries. In the Sasanian period (224–651 CE), they created a special alphabet to write it down.

In the modern era, Parsi scholars and Western researchers have studied the Avestan language and history.

History

Origins

Zarathushtra preached in ancient eastern Iran or Central Asia. His teachings emphasized:

  • one supreme God
  • truth and justice
  • free choice
  • rejection of violent rituals and corrupt priests
  • the coming judgment

The Persian Empires

Zoroastrianism became central in ancient Iran.

Achaemenid Empire (c. 550–330 BCE)

Founded by Cyrus the Great. Darius I and later kings honored Ahura Mazda, and Darius's inscriptions name Ahura Mazda as the source of his rule. Historians debate how fully the early Achaemenid kings followed Zarathushtra's teaching.

Parthian Empire (247 BCE–224 CE)

Zoroastrian practice continued.

Sasanian Empire (224–651 CE)

Zoroastrianism became the official state religion. Fire temples flourished, and the Avesta was written down.

Sacred Places

Important places include:

  • Yazd, Iran, home of an Atash Behram whose fire tradition holds has burned for over 1,500 years
  • Chak Chak, a mountain shrine near Yazd
  • Udvada, Gujarat, India, home of the Iranshah Atash Behram, which has burned since the Parsis arrived in India
  • Mumbai's fire temples

Decline and Migration

After the Arab conquest of Iran in the 7th century, Zoroastrians gradually became a minority as most Iranians converted to Islam.

According to the Qissa-i Sanjan, a group of Zoroastrians traveled to India and arrived at Sanjan in Gujarat. A local ruler welcomed them on certain conditions. They became the Parsi community.

The Parsis later flourished, especially in Mumbai under British rule, in business, industry, and philanthropy.

Major Historical Milestones

  • c. 1500–1000 BCE (dates debated): Life of Zarathushtra.
  • c. 550–330 BCE: Achaemenid Empire; Cyrus the Great.
  • 539 BCE: Cyrus conquers Babylon and allows exiled peoples, including the Jews, to return home.
  • 224–651 CE: Sasanian Empire; Zoroastrianism is the state religion.
  • 7th century: Arab conquest of Iran.
  • c. 8th–10th centuries: Parsis migrate to India.
  • 9th–10th centuries: Middle Persian texts such as the Denkard and Bundahishn are written.
  • 18th–19th centuries: Parsis rise to prominence in Mumbai.
  • 1854: The Persian Zoroastrian Amelioration Fund is founded to help Zoroastrians in Iran.
  • 20th century: Migration to North America, Britain, and Australia.
  • 21st century: Communities worldwide work to preserve the faith and address declining numbers.

Major Historical Figures

Important figures include:

  • Zarathushtra
  • King Vishtaspa
  • Cyrus the Great
  • Darius I
  • Ardashir I, founder of the Sasanian Empire
  • Kartir, a Sasanian high priest
  • Dadabhai Naoroji, early Indian nationalist and British MP
  • Jamsetji Tata, industrialist
  • Sir Dorabji Tata
  • Homi Bhabha, nuclear physicist
  • many priests, scholars, merchants, and philanthropists across Zoroastrian history

Traditions and Communities

Parsis

The Parsi community of India has preserved ancient rituals and priestly traditions. Parsis are known for education, business, and charity.

Iranian Zoroastrians

Iranian Zoroastrians have kept their faith through centuries as a minority. Iran's constitution recognizes Zoroastrians as a religious minority with a reserved seat in parliament.

Traditional and Reformist Interpretations

Zoroastrians today differ on:

  • accepting converts
  • children of interfaith marriages
  • funeral practices
  • the role of ritual purity
  • the role of women as priests (some communities now train women as mobedyars)
  • the authority of later texts compared with the Gathas

Regional Differences

India

Parsi traditions include Gujarati language, specific foods, and strict temple access rules.

Iran

Iranian Zoroastrians celebrate festivals such as Sadeh and keep their own customs.

Diaspora

In North America, Parsis and Iranians often worship together in Dar-e Mehrs.

United States

American Zoroastrians include Parsis from India and Pakistan, Iranians, and some converts. FEZANA, the Federation of Zoroastrian Associations of North America, connects communities.

Cultural Practices Versus Religious Requirements

It is important to distinguish between Zoroastrian teaching and Parsi or Iranian culture.

Examples of cultural practices that are not core religious requirements include:

  • specific Parsi foods
  • Gujarati language
  • particular wedding customs
  • regional dress

Myths, Common Misconceptions, and Facts

Myth: Zoroastrians worship fire.
Fact: Zoroastrians pray in the presence of fire as a symbol of God's light and purity. They worship Ahura Mazda.

Myth: Zoroastrianism has two equal gods.
Fact: Zoroastrianism teaches that Ahura Mazda is the one supreme God. Evil is real but is not equal to God and will be defeated.

Myth: Zoroastrianism is extinct.
Fact: Zoroastrianism is a living religion practiced by communities in India, Iran, and around the world.

Myth: Zoroastrianism is the same as ancient Persian culture.
Fact: Zoroastrianism is a religion. Iranian culture includes many traditions from Zoroastrian, Islamic, and other sources.

Myth: All Zoroastrians use Towers of Silence.
Fact: Traditional Parsis in India use dakhmas (Towers of Silence), where bodies are exposed to the sun and birds. Many Zoroastrians today, including in Iran and the diaspora, use burial or cremation.

Religion and Everyday Life

Family

Family is central to Zoroastrian life.

Zoroastrians are encouraged to maintain:

  • respect for parents
  • marriage
  • care for children
  • care for elderly relatives
  • remembrance of ancestors

Marriage

Marriage is a religious duty and a joyful event.

Children

Children are initiated into the faith through the Navjote (in India) or Sedreh-pushi (in Iran), usually between ages 7 and 15. The child puts on the sudreh and kusti for the first time.

Parents are expected to provide:

  • care
  • education
  • protection
  • moral guidance
  • affection

Education

Education is highly valued. Parsis were pioneers of education in India, including for girls.

Food

Zoroastrianism does not have strict dietary laws. Many Zoroastrians eat meat, though some avoid it on certain days. Good food is enjoyed as part of a happy life.

Clothing

Initiated Zoroastrians wear the sudreh (a white undershirt) and the kusti (a cord tied around the waist), which they untie and retie during prayers.

Many wear a head covering during prayer. Priests wear white.

Gender Roles

Zarathushtra addressed both men and women in his teachings. Both are equally responsible for choosing good.

Traditionally only men were priests. Today, women have been trained as assistant priests (mobedyars) in Iran and North America.

Work

Hard work is a religious virtue. Farming and productive work are praised in the Avesta as helping the good creation.

Business Ethics

Zoroastrian business ethics emphasize:

  • honesty
  • keeping contracts
  • fair dealing
  • charity

Mithra, the divine being of covenant, represents faithfulness to agreements.

Charity

Charity is integrated into Zoroastrian life. Parsi trusts provide housing, healthcare, and education.

Medicine and Healthcare

Zoroastrianism values health and healing. Ancient texts mention three kinds of healing: by herbs, by the knife (surgery), and by holy words.

Parsi philanthropists founded many hospitals.

Environment

Zoroastrianism teaches respect for the elements: fire, water, earth, and air. Polluting them is considered sinful. This has led many to describe Zoroastrianism as an early environmental faith.

Death and Funerals

Traditional Zoroastrian funeral practice includes:

  • prayers
  • a dog's gaze (sagdid), believed to drive away impurity
  • exposure of the body in a dakhma (Tower of Silence) in traditional Parsi practice
  • prayers for the soul for four days
  • annual remembrance

The aim is to avoid polluting fire, earth, and water. Because vultures have declined in India, some communities use solar concentrators, burial, or cremation.

Holidays

The major Zoroastrian observances are Nowruz, the Gahambars, the Muktad, Khordad Sal, and Zartosht no Diso.

Community Life

Community life commonly centers around:

  • fire temples
  • community halls
  • youth groups
  • charitable trusts
  • festivals

Politics and Civic Participation

Zoroastrians today live as minorities and participate in civic life. Parsis such as Dadabhai Naoroji and Pherozeshah Mehta were leaders of India's independence movement.

Relationships With People of Other Faiths

Zoroastrianism has a long history of interaction with:

  • Jews
  • Christians
  • Muslims
  • Hindus
  • Buddhists

The Hebrew Bible speaks of Cyrus with honor, calling him God's "anointed" (Isaiah 45:1).

Zoroastrianism in America

Zoroastrians began settling in North America in large numbers in the late 20th century. They have built Dar-e Mehrs and community centers, and participate in medicine, engineering, business, law, and education.

Zoroastrianism and Humanity

Ethical Monotheism

Zoroastrianism was among the earliest religions to teach one supreme good God, individual moral choice, and final judgment.

Human Rights

The Cyrus Cylinder, from 539 BCE, has been described by some as an early declaration of tolerance, though historians debate this description.

Philanthropy

Parsi philanthropy shaped modern India's education, science, and industry.

Zoroastrianism and Knowledge

Zoroastrianism values the Good Mind (Vohu Manah) and the use of wisdom in choosing right.

Zoroastrianism and Justice

Asha (truth and righteousness) is the central value of Zoroastrianism. Justice means living according to asha.

Zoroastrianism and Human Dignity

Every person is a creation of Ahura Mazda and has free will and moral responsibility.

Zoroastrianism as Thought, Word, and Deed

Zoroastrianism presents religion as:

Humata

Good thoughts.

Hukhta

Good words.

Hvarshta

Good deeds.

This framework can be useful for understanding Zoroastrianism as right intention + right speech + right action.

Conclusion

Zoroastrianism is one of the oldest living religions, centered on Ahura Mazda, the teachings of Zarathushtra, and the struggle between truth and falsehood.

At its theological core are:

  • one supreme God
  • the prophet Zarathushtra
  • free will
  • the struggle of good and evil
  • judgment after death
  • the final renewal of the world

At its practical core are:

  • prayer, often before fire
  • the sudreh and kusti
  • purity
  • festivals
  • charity

At its ethical core are:

  • good thoughts, good words, good deeds
  • truth
  • justice
  • care for the environment
  • hard work
  • generosity

Though small in number today, Zoroastrians have made major contributions to religion, ethics, government, and philanthropy.
