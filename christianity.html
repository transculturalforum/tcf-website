Christianity

A monotheistic faith centered on the life, teachings, death, and resurrection of Jesus of Nazareth, profoundly shaping global history and culture.

Introduction

Christianity is a monotheistic religion centered on the life, teachings, death, and resurrection of Jesus of Nazareth, whom Christians recognize as the Christ (Messiah), Son of God, and Lord.

Christianity emerged in the first century CE in the Jewish world of Roman-ruled Judea and Galilee. Its foundational scriptures are the Old Testament and New Testament, together forming the Christian Bible.

The Heart of Historic Christian Faith

At the heart of historic Christian faith is the belief that:

  • there is one God
  • God is understood as Father, Son, and Holy Spirit
  • Jesus Christ is the incarnate Son of God
  • Jesus was crucified
  • Jesus rose from the dead
  • salvation is offered through Christ
  • the Holy Spirit is active in the life of believers and the Church
  • humanity will ultimately face resurrection and judgment

The Nicene Creed, accepted across most historic Christian traditions, summarizes many of these central beliefs, including belief in one God, Jesus Christ, the Holy Spirit, the Church, baptism, resurrection, and eternal life.

Christianity is today the world's largest religious tradition by number of adherents. Pew Research Center estimates that there were approximately 2.3 billion Christians worldwide in 2020, representing about 28.8% of the world's population.

Christianity at a Glance

Name

Christianity. A follower is called a Christian.

The name derives from Christ, the Greek form of the Hebrew/Aramaic concept of Messiah, meaning "anointed one."

The earliest followers of Jesus were initially known as disciples or followers of "the Way." The term "Christian" became associated with the movement in the first century.

Approximate Number of Adherents

Pew Research Center's most recent comprehensive global religious estimates place the Christian population at approximately 2.3 billion people worldwide in 2020. Christians therefore represented approximately 29% of the world's population.

Christianity is geographically diverse. Historically concentrated in Europe and the Middle East, Christianity is now particularly large in:

  • Africa
  • Latin America
  • North America
  • Europe
  • parts of Asia
  • Oceania

Sub-Saharan Africa had surpassed Europe as the region containing the largest number of Christians by 2020.

Where Christianity Is Practiced

Christian communities exist throughout the world. Major Christian populations are found in:

  • United States

Christianity is therefore a global religion rather than a European religion.

Major Branches and Traditions

The principal historical families are:

Catholic Christianity

Centered institutionally on the Catholic Church, whose bishop of Rome is the Pope.

Eastern Orthodox Christianity

A communion of autocephalous churches including:

  • Greek Orthodox
  • Russian Orthodox
  • Serbian Orthodox
  • Romanian Orthodox
  • Antiochian Orthodox
  • Georgian Orthodox
  • Bulgarian Orthodox
  • other Orthodox churches

Oriental Orthodox Christianity

Includes churches such as:

  • Coptic Orthodox
  • Armenian Apostolic
  • Syriac Orthodox
  • Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo
  • Eritrean Orthodox Tewahedo
  • Malankara Orthodox Syrian traditions

These churches separated from the Byzantine imperial church following fifth-century Christological controversies.

Protestant Christianity

Emerging primarily from the sixteenth-century Reformation. Major Protestant traditions include:

  • Lutheran
  • Reformed/Calvinist
  • Anglican
  • Baptist
  • Methodist
  • Presbyterian
  • Congregational
  • Pentecostal
  • Evangelical
  • Adventist
  • non-denominational churches

There are thousands of Christian denominations and independent church bodies worldwide.

  • Trinity: Father, Son, and Holy Spirit
  • Jesus Christ: Central figure of Christianity
  • Christ: Messiah/Anointed One
  • Gospel: "Good news"; also the four canonical accounts of Jesus
  • Bible: Christian Scriptures
  • Old Testament: Scriptures largely shared with Judaism, with differences in canon
  • New Testament: Christian writings concerning Jesus and the early Church
  • Incarnation: God the Son taking human nature
  • Crucifixion: Jesus' execution on the cross
  • Resurrection: Jesus' rising from the dead
  • Salvation: Reconciliation with God and deliverance from sin and death

  • Grace: God's unearned favor

  • Repentance: Turning away from sin toward God

  • Eucharist/Communion: Central Christian sacramental/ritual meal

  • Holy Spirit: The third person of the Trinity in mainstream Christian theology

  • Disciple: Follower of Jesus
  • Amen: "Truly" or "so be it"
  • Hallelujah: "Praise the Lord"

Basic Christian Beliefs

Most historic Christian traditions affirm:

  1. One God
  2. The Trinity
  3. Jesus Christ as the Son of God
  4. The Incarnation
  5. Jesus' virgin birth
  6. Jesus' ministry and teachings
  7. Jesus' crucifixion
  8. Jesus' bodily resurrection
  9. Jesus' ascension
  10. The Holy Spirit
  11. Forgiveness of sins
  12. Resurrection of the dead
  13. Final judgment
  14. Eternal life

The precise theological explanation of these doctrines differs among Christian traditions.

Christmas

Celebrates the birth of Jesus.

Easter

Celebrates the resurrection of Jesus. For most Christians, Easter is the central annual celebration of the Christian faith.

Good Friday

Commemorates the crucifixion of Jesus.

Holy Week

The week surrounding the final events of Jesus' earthly ministry. It generally includes:

  • Palm Sunday
  • Maundy Thursday
  • Good Friday
  • Holy Saturday
  • Easter Sunday

Pentecost

Celebrates the coming of the Holy Spirit upon the disciples, described in Acts 2.

Core Beliefs

God

Christianity teaches that there is one God. Mainstream historic Christianity understands this one God as existing eternally as:

  • Father
  • Son
  • Holy Spirit

This is known as the Trinity. The Trinity does not mean three gods. The historic doctrine is one divine being or essence in three distinct persons. The Catholic Catechism summarizes the doctrine explicitly as one God in three persons.

Eastern Orthodox, Oriental Orthodox, Catholic, Anglican, and most Protestant traditions affirm Trinitarian Christianity. Some groups historically identified as Christian reject or reinterpret the Trinity; these are generally classified separately from Nicene Christianity.

Jesus Christ

Jesus is the central figure of Christianity. Historic Christianity teaches that Jesus is:

  • Messiah
  • Son of God
  • Lord
  • Savior
  • Word of God
  • fully divine
  • fully human

The doctrine of the Incarnation teaches that the eternal Son of God took human nature. The Gospel of John expresses the doctrine through the statement that "the Word became flesh."

The Catholic Catechism describes the Incarnation as the Son of God assuming human nature while remaining truly God. Eastern and Oriental Orthodox churches and mainstream Protestant traditions likewise affirm the Incarnation, although theological terminology and emphasis vary.

The Holy Spirit

The Holy Spirit is the third person of the Trinity in historic Christian theology. Christian traditions understand the Holy Spirit as:

  • God's presence
  • giver of life
  • source of spiritual gifts
  • guide of believers
  • sanctifier
  • active in the Church
  • active in Christian conversion and spiritual transformation

The Nicene Creed calls the Holy Spirit "the Lord, the giver of life."

Prophets and Biblical Figures

Christianity recognizes the prophets and major figures of the Hebrew Bible, including:

  • Adam
  • Noah
  • Abraham
  • Isaac
  • Jacob
  • Joseph
  • Moses
  • Aaron
  • Joshua
  • David
  • Solomon
  • Elijah
  • Isaiah
  • Jeremiah
  • Ezekiel
  • Daniel
  • John the Baptist

Jesus is understood not merely as another prophet but as the Messiah and Son of God.

Revelation

Christianity teaches that God reveals himself through creation, history, prophecy, Scripture, and supremely through Jesus Christ. The New Testament presents Jesus as the fullest revelation of God. Hebrews 1:1–2 describes God as having spoken in various ways through the prophets and ultimately through his Son.

Christian traditions differ regarding the relationship between:

  • Scripture
  • Church tradition
  • reason
  • theological authority

This difference becomes especially important when comparing Catholic, Orthodox, and Protestant Christianity.

Holy Scriptures

The Christian Bible has two major sections.

Old Testament: Contains writings corresponding substantially to the Hebrew Bible, although Christian traditions differ concerning which books are included, their order, and their classification.

New Testament: Contains 27 books accepted across Catholic, Orthodox, and Protestant Christianity, including:

  • Matthew
  • Mark
  • Luke
  • John
  • Acts
  • Paul's letters
  • other apostolic letters
  • Revelation

The four Gospels are the central narrative accounts of Jesus' life and ministry.

Angels and Spiritual Beings

Christianity teaches the existence of angels and other spiritual beings. Important biblical figures include:

  • Michael
  • Gabriel

Christian scripture also describes Satan and other evil spiritual beings. Christian traditions differ in the amount of theological emphasis placed on angels and demons.

Human Nature

Christianity teaches that human beings are created by God and possess inherent dignity. Genesis describes humanity as created in the image of God. This concept has historically provided an important foundation for Christian understandings of human dignity.

Christian theology also teaches that humanity has become affected by sin.

Sin

Sin refers broadly to rebellion against or separation from God's will. Christian traditions explain sin in somewhat different ways.

Catholic and Orthodox traditions emphasize both original sin and humanity's continuing need for grace, repentance, and transformation.

Many Protestant traditions emphasize humanity's fallen condition and the necessity of God's grace through Christ.

Christian theology distinguishes between:

  • original sin
  • personal sin
  • deliberate wrongdoing
  • sins of omission
  • sins against God
  • sins against other people

Repentance

Repentance means turning away from sin and turning toward God. The Greek New Testament term metanoia conveys a change of mind or direction.

Repentance is closely connected to:

  • forgiveness
  • faith
  • baptism
  • reconciliation
  • spiritual transformation

Prayer and Worship

Christian worship varies enormously. Common elements include:

  • prayer
  • Scripture reading
  • preaching
  • singing
  • hymns
  • confession
  • Communion/Eucharist
  • baptism
  • thanksgiving

Different traditions emphasize different forms. Catholic and Orthodox Christianity have highly developed liturgical traditions. Many Protestant churches emphasize preaching, congregational singing, Bible study, and spontaneous prayer.

Pentecostal churches may emphasize:

  • spiritual gifts
  • healing
  • tongues
  • prophetic prayer
  • highly expressive worship

Charity

Christianity places strong emphasis on caring for people in need. Jesus' teachings repeatedly emphasize:

  • feeding the hungry
  • helping the poor
  • caring for strangers
  • visiting prisoners
  • caring for the sick
  • serving vulnerable people

Matthew 25:31–46 is particularly important to Christian teachings about service to others.

Fasting

Fasting exists across Christianity, although its importance and form differ. Examples include:

  • Lent
  • Good Friday fasting
  • Orthodox fasting seasons
  • Catholic fasting
  • Protestant voluntary fasting
  • Pentecostal fasting

Eastern Orthodox Christians traditionally observe extensive fasting calendars. Many Protestant communities emphasize fasting as an individual spiritual discipline rather than a universally prescribed calendar.

Life

Christianity teaches that life is a gift from God. Human beings are called to:

  • love God
  • love others
  • seek righteousness
  • serve others
  • care for creation
  • develop Christian character

Jesus summarizes the two greatest commandments as love of God and love of neighbor.

Death

Christianity views death as a consequence of humanity's fallen condition but not the final destiny of humanity. Christian hope centers on resurrection.

Afterlife

Historic Christianity teaches:

  • death
  • resurrection
  • judgment
  • eternal life

The New Testament repeatedly emphasizes resurrection as fundamental to Christian faith. The Apostle Paul writes that if Christ has not been raised, Christian faith is futile; he then declares Christ's resurrection as the foundation of Christian hope. The resurrection of the dead is explicitly included in the Apostles' and Nicene Creeds.

Heaven and Eternal Life

Christians traditionally believe that those reconciled to God will experience eternal life with God. The New Testament uses imagery including:

  • God's kingdom
  • eternal life
  • resurrection
  • new creation
  • the presence of God

Christian traditions differ in how they explain:

  • intermediate states after death
  • heaven
  • purgatory
  • the millennium
  • the final state of creation

Hell and Punishment

Christianity traditionally teaches that final judgment may result in punishment or exclusion from God's kingdom. Christian traditions differ considerably concerning the nature of Hell. Views include:

  • eternal conscious punishment
  • separation from God
  • destruction/annihilation
  • various understandings of final judgment

Universalist Christians believe that ultimately all people will be reconciled to God, although this remains a minority position within historic Christianity.

Judgment

Christianity teaches that God will judge humanity. The New Testament describes a final judgment associated with the return of Christ. Judgment involves:

  • moral accountability
  • resurrection
  • justice
  • eternal consequences

Christian traditions differ over precisely how salvation, faith, works, grace, and judgment relate.

Free Will and Predestination

Christianity contains several major approaches.

Catholic: Generally emphasizes human freedom together with God's grace and sovereignty.

Eastern Orthodox: Strongly emphasizes human cooperation with divine grace, often described as synergy.

Reformed/Calvinist: Places greater emphasis on divine sovereignty and predestination.

Arminian/Wesleyan: Places greater emphasis on human response and free will while affirming God's grace.

Lutheran: Affirms salvation by grace through faith while maintaining distinctive teachings concerning human will and divine grace.

These differences have generated centuries of Christian theological debate.

Morality

Christian ethics emphasize:

  • love
  • forgiveness
  • mercy
  • justice
  • humility
  • honesty
  • sexual morality
  • care for the poor
  • faithfulness
  • protection of vulnerable people

Jesus' command, "Love your neighbor as yourself," is one of Christianity's most recognizable ethical principles. The Sermon on the Mount, particularly Matthew chapters 5–7, is central to Christian moral teaching.

Family

Christianity traditionally places great importance on family. Christian teaching emphasizes:

  • honoring parents
  • caring for children
  • fidelity
  • mutual responsibility
  • love
  • sacrifice
  • forgiveness

Different Christian traditions have different teachings concerning:

  • divorce
  • remarriage
  • contraception
  • gender roles
  • same-sex relationships
  • parenting

Marriage

Historic Christianity generally regards marriage as a covenantal relationship between a man and woman. However, contemporary Christian traditions differ significantly regarding same-sex marriage. Some churches maintain traditional definitions of marriage, while others recognize same-sex marriages.

Christian views concerning divorce also vary. Catholicism distinguishes divorce from annulment, while many Protestant traditions permit divorce under certain circumstances.

Community

The Christian community is commonly called the Church. The Greek term ekklesia refers to an assembly or congregation. Christians understand the Church in different ways:

  • Catholicism emphasizes a visible universal Church.
  • Eastern Orthodoxy emphasizes the communion of local Orthodox churches.
  • Protestant traditions vary from denominational structures to independent congregations.

Treatment of Others

Jesus teaches love toward one's neighbor and even one's enemies. The Sermon on the Mount contains teachings about:

  • forgiveness
  • reconciliation
  • generosity
  • non-retaliation
  • love of enemies
  • prayer
  • humility

Christians have interpreted these teachings differently in relation to:

  • self-defense
  • military service
  • criminal justice
  • political authority
  • war

Christian history contains both traditions of pacifism and traditions that developed theories of justified warfare.

Scriptures and Teachings

The Bible

The Bible is Christianity's foundational collection of sacred writings. It contains two major sections: the Old Testament and the New Testament.

The Bible contains multiple literary forms:

  • history
  • poetry
  • wisdom literature
  • prophecy
  • biography/Gospel
  • letters
  • apocalyptic literature

The Four Gospels

The four canonical Gospels are:

Matthew: Emphasizes Jesus as Messiah and presents extensive connections to Jewish Scripture.

Mark: The shortest Gospel and traditionally associated with a fast-moving account of Jesus' ministry.

Luke: Emphasizes themes including God's mercy, outsiders, the poor, women, prayer, and salvation.

John: Provides a distinctive theological presentation of Jesus, emphasizing his divine identity, signs, and relationship with the Father.

Acts of the Apostles

Acts describes the development of the early Christian movement after Jesus' resurrection and ascension. It emphasizes:

  • the apostles
  • Pentecost
  • the Holy Spirit
  • Peter
  • Paul
  • missionary expansion
  • early Christian communities

Paul's Letters

Paul's letters became foundational for Christian theology. Major themes include:

  • grace
  • faith
  • salvation
  • resurrection
  • Christian ethics
  • the Church
  • relationship between Jewish and Gentile believers

Important letters include:

  • Romans
  • 1 Corinthians
  • 2 Corinthians
  • Galatians
  • Ephesians
  • Philippians
  • Colossians
  • 1 Thessalonians

Oral and Written Tradition

Christianity developed through both oral and written transmission. Early Christians transmitted:

  • Jesus' sayings
  • stories of Jesus' actions
  • apostolic teaching
  • hymns
  • prayers
  • baptismal formulas
  • Eucharistic traditions

These traditions eventually became part of the New Testament and the wider tradition of the Church.

Creeds

The most important early Christian creeds include:

  • Apostles' Creed
  • Nicene Creed
  • Niceno-Constantinopolitan Creed
  • Chalcedonian Definition

The Nicene Creed is especially important for defining historic Christian beliefs about the Trinity and Christ.

History

Origins

Christianity emerged in the first century CE. Jesus was a Jewish teacher and preacher who lived in Roman-controlled Judea and Galilee. He gathered disciples and proclaimed the coming Kingdom of God.

His ministry included:

  • teaching
  • healing
  • parables
  • debates
  • prayer
  • ethical instruction
  • proclamation of God's kingdom

Jesus' Crucifixion

Jesus was crucified under the Roman governor Pontius Pilate. The crucifixion is central to Christian theology. Christians understand Jesus' death as having saving significance.

Different Christian traditions have developed different theological models explaining that significance, including:

  • atonement
  • sacrifice
  • victory over evil and death
  • reconciliation
  • substitution
  • moral transformation

Resurrection

The resurrection of Jesus is the central event of Christian faith. The New Testament presents the disciples as witnesses to the risen Christ. Paul identifies the resurrection as fundamental to Christian faith.

Historic Christianity therefore does not regard Jesus simply as a deceased teacher. Christians believe that Jesus conquered death and lives eternally. The resurrection is described as the foundation of Christian hope and faith.

Early Development

Following Jesus' death and resurrection, his followers established communities throughout:

  • Judea
  • Galilee
  • Samaria
  • Syria
  • Asia Minor
  • Greece
  • Rome
  • Egypt
  • North Africa

Paul played a particularly important role in spreading Christianity among Gentiles.

Expansion and Geographic Spread

Christianity spread rapidly throughout the Roman Empire. Major early centers included:

  • Jerusalem
  • Antioch
  • Alexandria
  • Rome
  • Constantinople

Christianity subsequently spread throughout:

  • Europe
  • North Africa
  • the Middle East
  • Ethiopia
  • Armenia
  • Persia
  • Central Asia
  • India

Much later, Christianity spread extensively throughout:

  • the Americas
  • sub-Saharan Africa
  • East Asia
  • Oceania

Major Historical Milestones

  • First century: Life of Jesus and formation of the earliest Christian communities.
  • c. 30–100 CE: Development of the apostolic Church and New Testament writings.
  • Second–third centuries: Expansion of Christianity throughout the Roman world.
  • 313 CE: Constantine's Edict of Milan established legal toleration for Christianity in the Roman Empire.
  • 325 CE: Council of Nicaea addressed major controversies concerning Christ's identity.
  • 380 CE: Christianity associated with Nicene Christianity became the official religion of the Roman Empire under Theodosius I.
  • 431 CE: Council of Ephesus.
  • 451 CE: Council of Chalcedon.
  • 1054: Traditional date associated with the division between Latin Western Christianity and Byzantine Eastern Christianity, although the separation developed gradually.
  • 1517: Martin Luther's protest against aspects of Western Church practice became a major catalyst for the Protestant Reformation.
  • 16th century: Protestant Reformation and Catholic Counter-Reformation.
  • 18th–19th centuries: Missionary expansion, evangelical movements, revivalism, and global Christian missions.
  • 20th century: Pentecostalism expanded dramatically.
  • 20th–21st centuries: Christianity's demographic center increasingly shifted toward Africa and parts of the Global South.

Major Divisions

Christianity's major historical divisions can be summarized as:

  1. Early Christianity
  2. Oriental Orthodox / Chalcedonian divisions
  3. Eastern and Western Christianity
  4. Roman Catholic / Eastern Orthodox separation
  5. Protestant Reformation
  6. Thousands of modern denominations and movements

This history should not be presented as though one side simply "created" the others. The divisions resulted from combinations of:

  • theology
  • politics
  • language
  • culture
  • geography
  • church authority
  • historical circumstances

Major Denominations and Schools

Roman Catholic Christianity

The Catholic Church is the world's largest single Christian communion. Distinctive features include:

  • Pope
  • bishops
  • seven sacraments
  • Eucharist
  • apostolic succession
  • Scripture and Sacred Tradition
  • Marian devotion
  • saints
  • purgatory

Catholic theology is extensively systematized in the Catechism of the Catholic Church.

Eastern Orthodox Christianity

Eastern Orthodoxy emphasizes:

  • apostolic tradition
  • liturgy
  • sacraments/mysteries
  • icons
  • bishops
  • ancient patriarchates
  • spiritual transformation

The Orthodox tradition does not accept papal universal jurisdiction in the Roman Catholic sense.

Oriental Orthodox Christianity

Includes:

  • Coptic Orthodox Church
  • Armenian Apostolic Church
  • Syriac Orthodox Church
  • Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church
  • Eritrean Orthodox Tewahedo Church
  • Malankara Orthodox Syrian traditions

These churches accept the first three ecumenical councils but did not accept the Council of Chalcedon.

Protestant Christianity

Protestantism emerged from the sixteenth-century Reformation. Major traditions include:

  • Lutheran: Associated historically with Martin Luther.
  • Reformed: Associated with figures including John Calvin and Ulrich Zwingli.
  • Anglican: Developed in England during the Reformation.
  • Baptist: Emphasizes believer's baptism and congregational principles.
  • Methodist: Developed from the Wesleyan revival.
  • Presbyterian: Historically associated with Reformed theology and Presbyterian church government.
  • Pentecostal: Emphasizes the Holy Spirit and spiritual gifts.
  • Evangelical: Emphasizes conversion, biblical authority, evangelism, and personal faith in Christ.

Regional Differences

Christianity looks very different around the world.

Africa: Christianity has experienced enormous growth, particularly in sub-Saharan Africa. African Christianity includes:

  • Catholic
  • Orthodox
  • Anglican
  • Protestant
  • Pentecostal
  • independent churches

Latin America: Historically dominated by Catholic Christianity, with major Protestant and Pentecostal growth.

Europe: Historically Christian but increasingly secular in many countries.

Middle East: Home to some of Christianity's oldest communities, including:

  • Copts
  • Maronites
  • Syriac Christians
  • Assyrians
  • Armenians
  • Greek Orthodox
  • other ancient communities

United States: Contains virtually every major Christian tradition.

Conservative and Progressive Interpretations

Christians disagree over numerous contemporary issues. These include:

  • abortion
  • contraception
  • same-sex relationships
  • gender roles
  • women's ordination
  • biblical interpretation
  • evolution
  • church-state relations

Different churches and denominations may reach very different conclusions while all identifying as Christian.

Cultural Christianity vs. Religious Christianity

A person may identify culturally as Christian without regularly practicing Christianity. Examples include people who:

  • celebrate Christmas
  • celebrate Easter
  • were baptized as children
  • identify with a Christian cultural heritage
  • rarely attend church

This distinction is particularly important in highly secular societies.

Myths, Common Misconceptions, and Facts

Myth: All Christians believe exactly the same thing.
Fact: Christianity contains enormous theological and institutional diversity. Catholic, Orthodox, Protestant, Oriental Orthodox, and independent Christian traditions differ on many doctrines and practices.

Myth: Christians have one identical Bible.
Fact: Christian traditions have different biblical canons. The New Testament is shared across mainstream Christian traditions, but the Old Testament contains different numbers of books in Catholic, Orthodox, and Protestant traditions.

Myth: Christianity teaches three gods.
Fact: Historic Christianity teaches one God in three persons. This is the doctrine of the Trinity.

Myth: Jesus is only a prophet in Christianity.
Fact: Historic Christianity teaches that Jesus is the incarnate Son of God. This is one of the most important differences between Christianity and Islam.

Myth: Christianity teaches that Jesus' death was the end.
Fact: The resurrection is central to Christianity. Christian faith teaches that Jesus rose from the dead.

Myth: Christians all worship in the same way.
Fact: Christian worship varies tremendously. A Catholic Mass, an Eastern Orthodox Divine Liturgy, an Anglican Eucharist, a Baptist service, and a Pentecostal worship service may look very different.

Myth: Christmas is explicitly commanded in the Bible.
Fact: The New Testament describes Jesus' birth but does not prescribe an annual Christmas festival. December 25 developed as a traditional Christian date for celebrating Jesus' birth.

Myth: Easter is simply another name for Passover.
Fact: They are historically related but distinct religious observances. Jesus' death and resurrection are narrated in the context of Passover in the New Testament, while Easter became Christianity's celebration of the resurrection.

Religion and Everyday Life

Family

Christian teachings traditionally emphasize:

  • love
  • fidelity
  • parental responsibility
  • care for children
  • honoring parents
  • forgiveness

Different churches have different teachings concerning family structure and gender roles.

Marriage

Most historic Christian traditions understand marriage as a covenant between a man and woman. Catholic and Orthodox Christianity generally treat marriage as sacramental or sacrament-like in distinctive ways.

Protestant traditions differ concerning:

  • remarriage
  • divorce
  • contraception
  • same-sex marriage

Some Protestant denominations now perform same-sex marriages while others explicitly reject the practice.

Children

Christian families commonly emphasize:

  • religious education
  • prayer
  • church attendance
  • moral formation
  • compassion
  • service

Some churches baptize infants. Others practice baptism only after an individual makes a personal profession of faith.

Education

Christianity has a long history of educational institutions. Christian communities established:

  • monasteries
  • schools
  • universities
  • seminaries
  • hospitals

Many of the world's oldest universities developed within Christian Europe.

Food

Unlike Islam and Judaism, Christianity generally does not maintain a universal system of halal/kosher dietary restrictions. However:

  • some Christians fast from certain foods
  • Orthodox Christians follow extensive fasting rules
  • Catholics have particular fasting and abstinence rules
  • individual denominations may have dietary practices

Alcohol is generally permitted in traditional Christianity, although some Christian movements advocate abstinence.

Clothing

Christianity does not generally prescribe one universal style of clothing. Some traditions encourage modesty. Certain Christian communities practice distinctive dress:

  • Amish
  • Mennonite
  • Orthodox Christian clergy
  • some conservative Protestant communities
  • religious orders

Gender Roles

Christianity contains several approaches.

Complementarian traditions teach differentiated roles for men and women.

Egalitarian traditions emphasize equal leadership opportunities.

Major Christian denominations differ over:

  • women as clergy
  • women as bishops
  • family leadership
  • political leadership
  • interpretation of Pauline texts

Work

Christian teaching generally views honest work positively. Important principles include:

  • honesty
  • diligence
  • fairness
  • service
  • care for workers
  • avoiding exploitation

The Christian tradition also contains extensive teachings concerning wealth and poverty.

Business Ethics

Christian ethics emphasize:

  • honesty
  • fair dealing
  • keeping promises
  • avoiding greed
  • paying workers fairly
  • helping the poor

Jesus' teachings frequently warn against allowing wealth to become more important than God.

Charity

Charity has been central to Christianity since its earliest centuries. Christian charitable institutions have historically included:

  • hospitals
  • orphanages
  • schools
  • homeless shelters
  • food banks
  • disaster relief organizations
  • international development organizations

The concept of Christian charity is closely connected to the command to love one's neighbor.

Medicine

Christian communities historically played major roles in establishing hospitals and charitable healthcare institutions. Christian medical ethics today address questions including:

  • abortion
  • euthanasia
  • organ transplantation
  • reproductive technology
  • genetic engineering
  • end-of-life care
  • contraception

Different Christian traditions reach different conclusions.

Death and Funerals

Christian funeral traditions vary. Common practices include:

  • burial
  • cremation
  • funeral services
  • Scripture readings
  • prayers
  • hymns
  • remembrance of the deceased

Catholic, Orthodox, Protestant, and independent churches have different funeral traditions.

Politics and Civic Participation

Christianity has no single modern political system. Historically, Christians have supported and opposed many different political systems.

Christian political thought has contributed to ideas concerning:

  • human dignity
  • social responsibility
  • charity
  • civil government
  • justice
  • rights
  • democracy
  • religious liberty

Christians today participate in:

  • conservative political movements
  • progressive political movements
  • centrist movements
  • nonpartisan civic organizations

There are major disagreements among Christians about how Christian ethics should influence public policy.

Relationships With Other Faiths

Christian attitudes toward other religions have changed considerably across history. Modern Christian traditions commonly emphasize some combination of:

  • religious freedom
  • peaceful coexistence
  • evangelism
  • interfaith dialogue
  • service to others

Catholic, Orthodox, Protestant, and evangelical traditions differ considerably in their theology of other religions.

Christianity has been central to American history. The United States contains communities representing virtually every major Christian tradition. American Christianity includes:

Christianity has made enormous contributions to:

  • hospitals

  • philosophy
  • literature

  • visual arts
  • architecture
  • law
  • social reform

Christian institutions were particularly important historically in the development of European universities and hospitals. Christian charitable organizations today operate globally.

Christianity and Human Dignity

Christian teaching about humanity being created in God's image has influenced Christian understandings of human dignity. The New Testament further emphasizes the equal spiritual value of believers before God.

Galatians 3:28 famously states that in Christ there is neither Jew nor Greek, slave nor free, male nor female, emphasizing unity in Christ. Christians have interpreted the implications of this principle differently in different historical contexts.

Christianity and Mercy

Mercy is a major Christian virtue. Jesus repeatedly teaches:

  • forgiveness
  • compassion
  • generosity
  • reconciliation
  • care for the poor
  • love of enemies

The parable of the Good Samaritan is particularly influential in Christian understandings of compassion toward others.

Christianity and Love

The concept of agape, often translated as self-giving or sacrificial love, is central to Christian theology. The New Testament's First Letter to the Corinthians contains the famous description of love in 1 Corinthians 13.

Jesus' command to love God and one's neighbor summarizes Christian ethics for many Christian traditions.

Christianity as Faith, Practice, and Community

Christianity can be understood through three interconnected dimensions:

Faith: Trust in God and belief in Jesus Christ.

Practice: Prayer, worship, Scripture, sacraments, charity, fasting, and ethical living.

Community: Participation in the Church and service to others.

Different traditions place different emphasis on these dimensions.

Christianity's Central Message

At its simplest, historic Christianity teaches:

  • God created humanity.
  • Humanity became separated from God through sin.
  • God acted in history to reconcile humanity to himself.
  • Jesus Christ is central to that reconciliation.
  • Jesus died and rose again.
  • Those who respond to God's grace through faith and repentance are offered forgiveness and new life.
  • Christians await the resurrection of the dead and the fulfillment of God's kingdom.

Different Christian traditions explain the mechanism and theology of salvation differently, but these themes form a common center of historic Christian belief.

Conclusion

Christianity is a global religious tradition centered on Jesus Christ.

Foundational Claims

  • one God
  • the Trinity
  • the divinity and humanity of Jesus
  • the Incarnation
  • Jesus' crucifixion
  • Jesus' resurrection
  • the Holy Spirit
  • forgiveness of sins
  • resurrection of the dead
  • final judgment
  • eternal life

Its central scriptures are the Old and New Testaments. Its central annual celebration is Easter, commemorating the resurrection of Jesus.

Ethical Teachings

  • love
  • mercy
  • forgiveness
  • justice
  • compassion
  • generosity
  • service
  • reconciliation

Its history extends over approximately two millennia and encompasses enormous theological, cultural, linguistic, and geographical diversity.

Christianity today is not one uniform institution. It encompasses Catholic, Eastern Orthodox, Oriental Orthodox, Protestant, Anglican, evangelical, Pentecostal, independent, and numerous other traditions. The global Christian population was approximately 2.3 billion in 2020, making Christianity the world's largest religious group by population at that time.

Understanding Christianity therefore requires distinguishing among Jesus' teachings, the Bible, early Christianity, Church tradition, theology, denominations, culture, and contemporary practice. That distinction matters because a practice found in one Christian denomination should not automatically be understood as a belief held by all Christians.

This page draws primarily on the foundational Christian sources: the Bible, the early creeds and councils, and the authoritative texts of the major Christian traditions.

New Testament. Particularly important:

Quotations on this page identify the book, chapter, and verse.

2. The Four Gospels

Matthew, Mark, Luke, and John receive special attention because they are the principal canonical accounts of Jesus' ministry.

3. Acts of the Apostles

Primary source for the early Christian movement and the first generations of the Church.

4. Paul's Letters

Especially:

  • Romans
  • 1 Corinthians
  • Galatians
  • Ephesians
  • Philippians

These are among the most important primary Christian texts for understanding early Christian theology.

5. Apostles' Creed

An early summary of Christian belief.

6. Nicene Creed

One of the most important historic statements of Christian faith, particularly concerning:

  • God
  • Jesus
  • Incarnation
  • resurrection
  • Holy Spirit
  • Church
  • baptism
  • eternal life

The text is preserved by the major historic Christian traditions.

7. Early Church Councils

For historical Christianity, the major councils are essential primary historical sources:

  • Council of Nicaea (325)
  • Council of Constantinople (381)
  • Council of Ephesus (431)
  • Council of Chalcedon (451)

They are particularly important for understanding the development of Christian doctrines concerning Jesus and the Trinity.

8. Catholic Sources

For Catholic Christianity: the Catechism of the Catholic Church (Vatican). The Catechism provides systematic Catholic teaching on:

  • Trinity
  • Jesus
  • salvation
  • sacraments
  • morality
  • prayer
  • Church
  • resurrection

For example, the Vatican's Catechism explicitly identifies the Trinity as the central mystery of Catholic faith and life.

9. Eastern Orthodox Sources

For Orthodox Christianity, separate sources are consulted:

  • writings of the Church Fathers
  • Ecumenical Councils
  • Divine Liturgy
  • Orthodox catechetical texts
  • writings of recognized Orthodox theologians

This avoids presenting Catholic formulations as though they automatically represent Eastern Orthodoxy.

10. Oriental Orthodox Sources

For Coptic, Armenian, Syriac, Ethiopian, Eritrean, and other Oriental Orthodox traditions, their own sources are used:

  • liturgical texts
  • theological writings
  • Church councils
  • catechisms
  • official Church sources

This is especially important because their Christological history differs from both Catholic/Orthodox and Protestant traditions.

11. Protestant Sources

Different Protestant traditions are represented by their own historical confessions. Examples include:

  • Augsburg Confession: Lutheran
  • Westminster Confession: Reformed/Presbyterian
  • Thirty-Nine Articles: Anglican
  • Baptist Faith and Message: Baptist
  • Methodist Articles of Religion: Methodist

These are denominational sources, not statements that every Christian accepts.
