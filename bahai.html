Bahá'í Faith

An independent world religion founded in 19th-century Persia that emphasizes the spiritual unity of all humankind.

Introduction

The Bahá'í Faith is a monotheistic religion founded in 19th-century Iran by Bahá'u'lláh (1817–1892). It is one of the world's youngest independent religions and has spread to almost every country.

Bahá'ís believe in one God, who has revealed His will to humanity through a series of divine messengers, including Abraham, Moses, Buddha, Zoroaster, Jesus, Muhammad, the Báb, and Bahá'u'lláh. They believe that these messengers taught one progressively unfolding religion, suited to the needs of each age.

The central teaching of the Bahá'í Faith is unity: the oneness of God, the oneness of religion, and the oneness of humanity. Bahá'ís believe that humanity is now moving toward a unified, peaceful global society.

Bahá'u'lláh wrote:

"The earth is but one country, and mankind its citizens."

Bahá'ís understand their faith not simply as a set of beliefs but as a way of life that includes prayer, service, education, community building, and work for social justice.

The Bahá'í Faith at a Glance

Name

The Bahá'í Faith

A follower is called a Bahá'í.

The name comes from Bahá, an Arabic word meaning "glory" or "splendor." Bahá'u'lláh means "the Glory of God."

The Bahá'í Faith began in an Islamic setting, but Bahá'ís regard it as an independent religion with its own scriptures, laws, and institutions.

Approximate Number of Adherents

There are an estimated 5 to 8 million Bahá'ís worldwide. Bahá'í sources often cite more than 5 million, while the World Religion Database estimates about 8 million.

Bahá'ís live in more than 200 countries and territories, making the Bahá'í Faith one of the most widely spread religions in the world, although small in number.

Where the Bahá'í Faith Is Practiced

Large Bahá'í communities are found in:

  • India
  • the United States
  • Kenya
  • Vietnam
  • the Democratic Republic of the Congo
  • the Philippines
  • Zambia
  • South Africa
  • Iran
  • Bolivia and other Latin American countries
  • Pacific Island nations

Iran, the birthplace of the faith, has an estimated 300,000 Bahá'ís, its largest religious minority, who face serious persecution.

The Bahá'í writings were revealed mainly in Arabic and Persian and have been translated into more than 800 languages.

Major Branches and Traditions

The Bahá'í Faith has remained unified, without denominations. Bahá'u'lláh set out a clear line of authority, called the Covenant:

  1. Bahá'u'lláh, the founder
  2. 'Abdu'l-Bahá, his son, the appointed interpreter
  3. Shoghi Effendi, the Guardian
  4. the Universal House of Justice, elected since 1963

Small groups that broke away from this line of succession are not recognized by the Bahá'í community.

Important Bahá'í Terminology

  • Bahá'u'lláh: "The Glory of God"; founder of the Bahá'í Faith
  • The Báb: "The Gate"; forerunner of Bahá'u'lláh
  • 'Abdu'l-Bahá: "Servant of Bahá"; son and successor of Bahá'u'lláh
  • Manifestation of God: A divine messenger who reveals God's will
  • Progressive revelation: The belief that God reveals religion in stages
  • Kitáb-i-Aqdas: "The Most Holy Book"; book of laws
  • Kitáb-i-Íqán: "The Book of Certitude"
  • Covenant: The line of authority in the faith
  • Universal House of Justice: The supreme governing council
  • Spiritual Assembly: Elected local or national council
  • Feast (Nineteen Day Feast): The community gathering every Bahá'í month
  • Mashriqu'l-Adhkár: House of Worship
  • Ridván: The holiest festival
  • Naw-Rúz: The Bahá'í New Year
  • Obligatory prayer: Daily prayer required of adult Bahá'ís
  • Huqúqu'lláh: "The Right of God"; a spiritual payment
  • Consultation: The Bahá'í method of group decision-making
  • Bahá'í World Centre: Spiritual and administrative center in Haifa and Akka, Israel

Basic Beliefs

Core Bahá'í beliefs include:

  1. the oneness of God
  2. the oneness of religion
  3. the oneness of humanity
  4. progressive revelation through Manifestations of God
  5. the immortality of the soul
  6. the harmony of science and religion

Important Bahá'í social principles include:

  1. elimination of all forms of prejudice
  2. equality of women and men
  3. universal education
  4. independent investigation of truth
  5. elimination of extremes of wealth and poverty
  6. a universal auxiliary language
  7. world peace and a world federation

Major Bahá'í Holidays

The Bahá'í calendar (Badí' calendar) has 19 months of 19 days, plus four or five extra days.

Naw-Rúz

The Bahá'í New Year, at the spring equinox (around March 20 or 21). It follows the 19-day fast.

Ridván

A twelve-day festival from about April 20 to May 2. It marks Bahá'u'lláh's declaration of his mission in Baghdad in 1863. The first, ninth, and twelfth days are holy days.

The Twin Holy Birthdays

The births of the Báb and Bahá'u'lláh, celebrated on consecutive days in October or November.

Other Holy Days

  • Declaration of the Báb (May)
  • Ascension of Bahá'u'lláh (May)
  • Martyrdom of the Báb (July)
  • Day of the Covenant and Ascension of 'Abdu'l-Bahá (November)

Bahá'ís suspend work on nine holy days of the year.

Core Beliefs

God

Bahá'ís believe in one God, the creator of the universe.

God is described as:

  • one
  • eternal
  • all-knowing
  • all-powerful
  • loving
  • just
  • beyond human understanding in essence

Bahá'u'lláh taught that God's essence is unknowable. People come to know God through His Manifestations, who reflect God's attributes like a perfect mirror reflects the sun.

The Bahá'í Faith rejects:

  • the idea that God has a physical form
  • the idea that God becomes incarnate
  • the idea that one religion alone has all truth for all time
  • prejudice of every kind

Manifestations of God

Bahá'ís believe that God sends Manifestations to humanity. They include:

  • Abraham
  • Moses
  • Krishna
  • Zoroaster
  • Buddha
  • Jesus
  • Muhammad
  • the Báb
  • Bahá'u'lláh

Each Manifestation brings spiritual truths that are eternal and social laws suited to the time.

Bahá'u'lláh is regarded as the Manifestation for this age. Bahá'ís believe he fulfills the promises of earlier religions. They do not believe he is the last Manifestation; another will come after at least 1,000 years.

Progressive Revelation

Bahá'ís believe that religious truth is revealed progressively, like a school in which humanity is taught more as it matures.

All the great religions come from the same God. Their differences reflect the needs of different times and places.

The Bahá'í Scriptures

The Bahá'í scriptures consist of the writings of the Báb and Bahá'u'lláh, and the authorized interpretations of 'Abdu'l-Bahá and Shoghi Effendi.

Bahá'u'lláh's key works include:

  • the Kitáb-i-Aqdas (The Most Holy Book): laws and institutions
  • the Kitáb-i-Íqán (The Book of Certitude): progressive revelation
  • the Hidden Words: short spiritual teachings
  • the Seven Valleys: the spiritual journey
  • Gleanings from the Writings of Bahá'u'lláh
  • the Tablets to the kings and rulers of the world

The writings contain:

  • prayers
  • spiritual teachings
  • laws
  • social principles
  • explanations of earlier scriptures
  • guidance for institutions

Bahá'u'lláh's writings are very extensive. The Bahá'í community considers all original writings to be authoritative.

Interpretation and Authority

'Abdu'l-Bahá was appointed by Bahá'u'lláh as the interpreter of his writings. After him, Shoghi Effendi was appointed Guardian.

Today the Universal House of Justice can legislate on matters not addressed in the writings. It cannot change the writings or give authoritative interpretations of them.

Individual Bahá'ís may share their own understanding, but it has no authority over others.

Spiritual Beings

The Bahá'í writings describe the spiritual world as beyond physical description.

Bahá'ís do not believe in Satan as a real being. 'Abdu'l-Bahá explained that evil is the absence of good, as darkness is the absence of light. The "devil" is a symbol of the lower nature of human beings.

Human Nature

Bahá'ís believe that each human being has a rational soul that comes into being at conception and is immortal.

Human beings have two natures:

  • a spiritual nature
  • a material or animal nature

The purpose of life is to develop the spiritual nature.

Bahá'u'lláh wrote that every person is "a mine rich in gems of inestimable value," and that education reveals these gems.

The Bahá'í Faith does not teach original sin or reincarnation.

Sin

Sin is turning away from God and following the lower nature.

Bahá'ís are encouraged to:

  • avoid backbiting
  • avoid alcohol and drugs
  • avoid gambling
  • avoid sexual relations outside marriage
  • avoid partisan politics

Repentance

Repentance is a private matter between the individual and God. Bahá'ís do not confess sins to any other person.

A person who has done wrong is encouraged to:

  1. recognize the wrong
  2. turn to God in prayer
  3. sincerely regret it
  4. seek forgiveness
  5. make amends where possible
  6. strive to improve

Prayer and Worship

Prayer is central to Bahá'í life.

Every Bahá'í aged 15 and over says one of three obligatory prayers daily:

  • the short prayer, once between noon and sunset
  • the medium prayer, three times a day
  • the long prayer, once a day

Bahá'ís also:

  • read from the sacred writings every morning and evening
  • repeat "Alláh-u-Abhá" ("God is Most Glorious") 95 times a day
  • pray for others
  • meditate and reflect

The Bahá'í Faith has no clergy. Worship is simple and has no sermons or set rituals beyond the reading of scripture and prayer.

Houses of Worship

Bahá'í Houses of Worship are open to people of all faiths. Each has nine sides and a central dome, symbolizing unity.

Famous examples include:

  • the Lotus Temple in New Delhi, India
  • the Bahá'í House of Worship in Wilmette, Illinois, USA
  • Houses of Worship in Panama, Australia, Uganda, Samoa, Germany, Chile, Cambodia, Colombia, and elsewhere

Charity and Service

Bahá'ís contribute voluntarily to the Bahá'í Fund. Only Bahá'ís may contribute.

Huqúqu'lláh ("the Right of God") is a spiritual obligation to pay 19% of surplus wealth after necessary expenses.

Service to humanity is seen as worship. 'Abdu'l-Bahá taught that work done in a spirit of service is worship.

Fasting

Bahá'ís aged 15 to 70 fast from sunrise to sunset for 19 days in March, ending at Naw-Rúz.

The fast is a time of:

  • prayer
  • reflection
  • spiritual renewal
  • self-discipline

Those who are ill, traveling, pregnant, nursing, or doing heavy labor are exempt.

Life

Bahá'ís regard life as a time for the soul to develop spiritual qualities.

Bahá'u'lláh wrote that humanity was created "to carry forward an ever-advancing civilization."

Human beings are expected to:

  • know and love God
  • develop virtues
  • serve humanity
  • work in a trade or profession
  • educate themselves and their children
  • work for unity
  • avoid prejudice

Death

Death is the soul's release from the body and its birth into the spiritual world.

'Abdu'l-Bahá compared life in this world to a child's life in the womb, which prepares it for a much greater world after birth.

Afterlife

Bahá'ís believe that the soul continues to progress after death, toward God, forever.

Heaven and Hell

The Bahá'í writings describe heaven and hell not as physical places but as states of the soul:

  • heaven is nearness to God
  • hell is distance from God

Judgment

Bahá'ís believe that each person is accountable to God. Bahá'u'lláh wrote: "Bring thyself to account each day ere thou art summoned to a reckoning."

God's justice is balanced by mercy, and Bahá'ís pray for the progress of the souls of the departed.

Free Will

Bahá'ís believe that human beings have free will to choose good or evil. Some things, such as birth, illness, and death, are beyond human control, but each person is responsible for their own choices.

Morality

Bahá'í ethics emphasize virtues such as:

  • truthfulness, which 'Abdu'l-Bahá called "the foundation of all human virtues"
  • honesty
  • trustworthiness
  • kindness
  • justice
  • chastity
  • courtesy
  • humility
  • generosity
  • service
  • avoiding backbiting

Bahá'u'lláh wrote in the Hidden Words:

"The best beloved of all things in My sight is Justice."

Family

Family is central to Bahá'í life and is seen as the foundation of society.

The Bahá'í Faith places particular importance on:

  • unity within the family
  • respect for parents
  • the education of children
  • equality between husband and wife

Marriage

Bahá'í marriage requires:

  • the consent of both partners
  • the consent of all living parents
  • a simple ceremony with the vow: "We will all, verily, abide by the Will of God"

Marriage is described as a "fortress for well-being." Bahá'ís may marry people of other faiths. Divorce is permitted but discouraged, and requires a "year of patience" first.

Community

Bahá'í community life centers around:

  • the Nineteen Day Feast
  • devotional gatherings
  • children's classes
  • junior youth groups
  • study circles
  • holy day celebrations

Bahá'í communities are governed by elected councils called Spiritual Assemblies.

Treatment of Others

Bahá'ís are asked to associate with people of all religions "in a spirit of friendliness and fellowship," as Bahá'u'lláh wrote.

Bahá'ís believe in the oneness of humanity and reject racism, nationalism, and religious prejudice.

Scriptures and Teachings

The Writings of Bahá'u'lláh

The writings of Bahá'u'lláh are the foundation of the Bahá'í Faith.

Their major themes include:

  • God
  • the Manifestations of God
  • the soul
  • unity
  • justice
  • world peace
  • prayer
  • spiritual growth
  • laws and institutions

The Writings of the Báb

The Báb's writings, including the Persian Bayán, prepared the way for Bahá'u'lláh.

The Writings of 'Abdu'l-Bahá and Shoghi Effendi

'Abdu'l-Bahá's works, such as Some Answered Questions and Paris Talks, explain Bahá'í teachings. Shoghi Effendi's works, such as God Passes By, explain history and institutions.

Bahá'í Administration

The Bahá'í administrative order answers practical questions such as:

  • How are decisions made?
  • How are leaders chosen?
  • How are funds managed?
  • How are community activities organized?

Bahá'í elections have no nominations or campaigning. Members vote by secret ballot for those they believe are best qualified.

Bahá'í Scholarship

The Bahá'í Faith has no clergy, so every believer is encouraged to study the writings directly. Bahá'í scholarship includes:

  • translation of the writings from Persian and Arabic
  • study of the history of the Báb and Bahá'u'lláh
  • study of the writings' relationship to other religions
  • work on social and economic development

Personal understanding is welcomed but is not authoritative. Authoritative interpretation belongs to 'Abdu'l-Bahá and Shoghi Effendi, and the Universal House of Justice rules on matters the writings do not address.

History

Origins

In 1844 in Shiraz, Iran, a young merchant named Siyyid 'Alí-Muhammad declared that he was the Báb, the "Gate," and that a greater messenger would soon appear.

The Báb's movement spread rapidly but was violently persecuted. The Báb was executed in Tabriz in 1850. Thousands of his followers, called Bábís, were killed.

Bahá'u'lláh

Mírzá Husayn-'Alí, known as Bahá'u'lláh, was born in Tehran in 1817 and was a follower of the Báb.

In 1852 he was imprisoned in the Síyáh-Chál ("Black Pit") dungeon in Tehran. There, he experienced the beginning of his revelation.

He was then exiled to:

  • Baghdad (1853)
  • Constantinople and Adrianople (1863–1868)
  • Akka, in the Ottoman province of Syria (now Israel), in 1868

In 1863, in the Garden of Ridván in Baghdad, he declared that he was the one promised by the Báb.

He wrote to the kings and rulers of the world calling for peace and justice.

He died near Akka in 1892.

'Abdu'l-Bahá and the Spread of the Faith

'Abdu'l-Bahá led the community from 1892 to 1921. He traveled to Europe and North America (1911–1913), speaking about peace, racial unity, and the equality of women and men.

Shoghi Effendi led the faith from 1921 to 1957. He developed the administrative institutions and helped spread the faith worldwide.

The Bahá'í World Centre

The spiritual and administrative center is in Haifa and Akka, Israel. It includes:

  • the Shrine of Bahá'u'lláh near Akka, the holiest Bahá'í site
  • the Shrine of the Báb in Haifa
  • the terraced gardens on Mount Carmel, a UNESCO World Heritage Site
  • the seat of the Universal House of Justice

Major Historical Milestones

  • 1844: Declaration of the Báb.
  • 1850: Execution of the Báb.
  • 1852: Bahá'u'lláh imprisoned in the Síyáh-Chál.
  • 1863: Bahá'u'lláh's declaration in the Garden of Ridván.
  • 1868: Bahá'u'lláh exiled to Akka.
  • 1873: Kitáb-i-Aqdas completed.
  • 1892: Death of Bahá'u'lláh.
  • 1893: First public mention of the faith in America, at the World's Parliament of Religions in Chicago.
  • 1912: 'Abdu'l-Bahá visits the United States and lays the foundation of the Wilmette House of Worship.
  • 1921–1957: Ministry of Shoghi Effendi.
  • 1963: First election of the Universal House of Justice.
  • 1979 onward: Severe persecution of Bahá'ís in Iran after the Islamic Revolution.
  • 21st century: Growth of community-building activities worldwide.

Major Historical Figures

Important figures include:

  • the Báb
  • Bahá'u'lláh
  • 'Abdu'l-Bahá
  • Shoghi Effendi
  • Táhirih, poet and early advocate for women's rights
  • Mullá Husayn, first to believe in the Báb
  • Bahíyyih Khánum, daughter of Bahá'u'lláh
  • Martha Root, traveling teacher
  • Louis Gregory, African American Bahá'í leader
  • Ruhiyyih Khanum
  • many teachers, pioneers, and martyrs

Community and Diversity

Unity of the Community

The Bahá'í Faith has no denominations. All Bahá'ís follow the same writings and institutions.

Diversity of Members

Bahá'ís come from almost every ethnic, national, and religious background. Many communities include people who were previously Christian, Muslim, Hindu, Buddhist, Jewish, or of no religion.

Traditional and Modern Interpretations

The Bahá'í Faith does not divide into traditional and modern branches. Its writings name who may interpret them with authority: 'Abdu'l-Bahá and Shoghi Effendi. The Universal House of Justice, elected every five years, decides questions the writings do not cover.

Within that framework, Bahá'ís are free to hold personal understandings of the writings, and communities apply its principles in ways that fit their own time and culture.

Regional Differences

India

Home to one of the largest Bahá'í populations and the Lotus Temple.

Africa

Large and growing communities in Kenya, Zambia, Congo, and elsewhere.

Iran

The historical birthplace of the faith, where Bahá'ís face persecution.

United States

American Bahá'ís include people of every background, with an especially strong history of racial unity work.

Cultural Practices Versus Religious Requirements

Bahá'ís are encouraged to keep their own cultures where these agree with Bahá'í principles. Food, dress, music, and celebration styles vary widely by country.

Myths, Common Misconceptions, and Facts

Myth: The Bahá'í Faith is a sect of Islam.
Fact: The Bahá'í Faith began in an Islamic setting but is an independent religion with its own scriptures, laws, and institutions.

Myth: Bahá'ís believe all religions are exactly the same.
Fact: Bahá'ís believe all religions come from one God and share spiritual truths, but they differ in social laws suited to their times.

Myth: The Bahá'í Faith is a cult.
Fact: The Bahá'í Faith is an established world religion with no clergy, a democratically elected administration, and members in over 200 countries.

Myth: Bahá'ís try to aggressively convert others.
Fact: Bahá'ís share their faith, but aggressive proselytizing is forbidden.

Myth: Bahá'ís are involved in politics.
Fact: Bahá'ís do not join political parties or take sides in partisan politics, though they vote and serve their societies.

Religion and Everyday Life

Family

Family unity is central to Bahá'í life.

Bahá'ís are encouraged to maintain:

  • respect for parents
  • marriage
  • care for children
  • care for elderly relatives
  • consultation within the family

Marriage

Marriage is a spiritual and social union based on mutual consent and parental consent.

Children

Bahá'ís place strong emphasis on the education of children.

Parents are expected to provide:

  • care
  • education
  • moral and spiritual guidance
  • affection

'Abdu'l-Bahá taught that if a family cannot educate all its children, daughters should be given priority, because mothers are the first educators.

Education

Universal education is a core Bahá'í principle. Bahá'ís run schools and educational programs in many countries.

Food

Bahá'ís have no dietary laws except avoiding alcohol and drugs (unless prescribed). Food customs follow local culture.

Clothing

Bahá'ís have no religious dress. They are encouraged to dress modestly and cleanly.

Gender Roles

The equality of women and men is a central principle. 'Abdu'l-Bahá compared humanity to a bird with two wings, men and women, that cannot fly unless both are strong.

Women serve on all Bahá'í institutions except the Universal House of Justice, which is limited to men. Bahá'ís believe the reason for this will become clear in the future.

Work

Work done in a spirit of service is considered worship. Bahá'u'lláh made it obligatory for each person to engage in a trade or profession. Begging is forbidden.

Business Ethics

Bahá'í business ethics emphasize:

  • trustworthiness
  • honesty
  • fair dealing
  • avoiding exploitation
  • fair and moderate interest, which Bahá'u'lláh permitted in his Tablet of Ishráqát

Charity

Bahá'ís contribute to the Bahá'í funds and to social and economic development projects in their communities.

Medicine and Healthcare

Bahá'u'lláh instructed his followers to consult competent physicians. Bahá'ís accept modern medicine. Organ donation is permitted.

Death and Funerals

Bahá'í funeral practice includes:

  • washing and wrapping the body
  • a ring with a sacred verse
  • a coffin of durable material
  • the Prayer for the Dead (for adults)
  • burial within one hour's journey of the place of death
  • the body facing toward the Shrine of Bahá'u'lláh

Cremation is not permitted.

Holidays

The major Bahá'í holy days are Naw-Rúz, Ridván, the Twin Holy Birthdays, the Declaration of the Báb, and the commemorations of the passing of the central figures.

Community Life

Bahá'í community life commonly centers around:

  • devotional gatherings
  • children's classes
  • junior youth groups
  • study circles
  • Nineteen Day Feasts

These activities are open to everyone.

Politics and Civic Participation

Bahá'ís are required to obey the laws of their country and to avoid partisan politics.

Bahá'ís vote, serve in public service, and work for social good. The Bahá'í International Community has consultative status at the United Nations.

Relationships With People of Other Faiths

Bahá'u'lláh taught Bahá'ís to "consort with the followers of all religions in a spirit of friendliness and fellowship."

Bahá'ís are active in interfaith work. World Religion Day was started by the U.S. Bahá'í community in 1950.

The Bahá'í Faith in America

The Bahá'í Faith came to America in the 1890s. 'Abdu'l-Bahá visited in 1912 and spoke at churches, synagogues, universities, and Black institutions such as Howard University.

American Bahá'ís have a long history of work for racial unity. The House of Worship in Wilmette, Illinois, was completed in 1953.

The Bahá'í Faith and Humanity

Social Development

Bahá'ís run thousands of community development and education projects worldwide.

Architecture

Bahá'í Houses of Worship and the terraces on Mount Carmel are known for their architecture and gardens.

International Cooperation

Bahá'u'lláh called for collective security and world governance well before the League of Nations and the United Nations.

The Bahá'í Faith and Knowledge

The Bahá'í writings teach that science and religion must agree. 'Abdu'l-Bahá taught that religion contrary to science is superstition.

The Bahá'í Faith and Justice

Bahá'u'lláh called justice "the best beloved of all things" in God's sight. Bahá'ís believe justice is necessary for unity.

The Bahá'í Faith and Human Dignity

Bahá'ís believe in the oneness of humanity. Bahá'u'lláh wrote: "Ye are the fruits of one tree, and the leaves of one branch."

This provides a Bahá'í foundation for:

  • racial unity
  • gender equality
  • religious tolerance
  • world peace

The Bahá'í Faith and Unity

Unity is the central theme of the Bahá'í Faith. Bahá'u'lláh wrote: "The well-being of mankind, its peace and security, are unattainable unless and until its unity is firmly established."

The Bahá'í Faith as Worship, Service, and Unity

The Bahá'í Faith presents religion as:

Worship

Prayer, meditation, and study of the writings.

Service

Service to humanity, seen as a form of worship.

Unity

Building unity in family, community, and the world.

This framework can be useful for understanding the Bahá'í Faith as devotion + service + unity.

Current Challenges

The most severe challenge facing the Bahá'í Faith today is the intense, state-sponsored persecution of the Bahá'í community in Iran (and increasingly in Yemen). In Iran, Bahá'ís are routinely imprisoned for their beliefs, their businesses are closed, their cemeteries are bulldozed, and their youth are banned from attending universities.

Globally, a primary challenge is navigating community building and social action while adhering strictly to the Bahá'í law of non-involvement in partisan politics, especially in highly polarized modern societies.

Conclusion

The Bahá'í Faith is a global monotheistic religion centered on the oneness of God, religion, and humanity.

At its theological core are:

  • one God
  • progressive revelation
  • the Manifestations of God
  • the immortal soul
  • the Covenant

At its practical core are:

  • daily prayer
  • the annual fast
  • the Nineteen Day Feast
  • community-building activities
  • service

At its ethical core are:

  • unity
  • justice
  • truthfulness
  • equality of women and men
  • elimination of prejudice
  • service to humanity

Although relatively small, the Bahá'í community is spread across almost every country and reflects humanity's diversity.
