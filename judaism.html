Judaism

An ancient monotheistic religion representing the cultural, legal, and spiritual heritage of the Jewish people, centered around a covenant with God.

Introduction

Judaism is one of the world's oldest monotheistic religions. It is the religion of the Jewish people and traces its origins to Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob, and to the covenant between God and the people of Israel at Mount Sinai.

Jews believe that there is one God, who created the universe, who acts in history, and who gave the Torah to the Jewish people as a guide for life. Judaism understands itself not simply as a set of beliefs but as a covenant relationship with God expressed through study, prayer, ethical conduct, family life, community, and the observance of commandments (mitzvot).

Judaism is both a religion and a people. A person may be Jewish by birth or by conversion, and Jewish identity includes religious, cultural, ethnic, and historical dimensions.

The central declaration of Jewish faith is the Shema:

"Hear, O Israel: the Lord is our God, the Lord is One." (Deuteronomy 6:4)

The Shema is recited in morning and evening prayers and is traditionally among the last words a Jew says before death.

The Hebrew Bible presents the basic message of Judaism as the love and service of the one God, and justice and kindness toward others. The prophet Micah summarized it this way:

"Do justice, love kindness, and walk humbly with your God." (Micah 6:8)

Judaism at a Glance

Name

Judaism

A follower of Judaism is called a Jew. The collective is often called the Jewish people.

The word "Jew" comes from Judah, one of the twelve tribes of Israel and the name of the southern kingdom of ancient Israel. The Hebrew term for the people is Am Yisrael, "the people of Israel."

Jews commonly refer to God by several names. Out of reverence, the four-letter name of God (YHWH) is not pronounced. In prayer Jews say Adonai ("my Lord"), and in conversation many say HaShem ("the Name"). Some Jews write "G-d" in English out of respect.

Approximate Number of Adherents

According to Pew Research Center, there were approximately 14.8 million Jews worldwide in 2020, about 0.2% of the world's population.

Estimates vary depending on how Jewish identity is defined, for example whether people who have a Jewish parent but do not practice Judaism are counted. Some estimates that use broader definitions are higher.

Where Judaism Is Practiced

Nearly 80% of the world's Jews live in two countries: Israel and the United States.

Other significant Jewish communities are found in:

  • France
  • Canada
  • the United Kingdom
  • Argentina
  • Russia
  • Germany
  • Australia
  • Brazil
  • South Africa
  • Ukraine
  • Mexico

Jews speak many languages. Hebrew has a special religious position because it is the language of the Hebrew Bible and of prayer. Aramaic is the language of much of the Talmud. Jewish communities also developed their own languages, such as Yiddish and Ladino.

Major Branches and Traditions

Modern Judaism includes several major movements:

Orthodox Judaism

Holds that the Written and Oral Torah were given by God and that Jewish law (halakha) is binding. It includes Modern Orthodox and Haredi ("ultra-Orthodox") communities, including Hasidic groups.

Conservative (Masorti) Judaism

Holds that Jewish law is binding but develops over time through scholarly interpretation.

Reform (Progressive / Liberal) Judaism

Emphasizes ethical teachings and personal autonomy. It treats traditional law as a source of guidance rather than as strictly binding.

Reconstructionist Judaism

Views Judaism as an evolving religious civilization of the Jewish people.

Many Jews do not belong to any movement. Some identify as secular or cultural Jews.

Jews are also often described by their ancestral community:

  • Ashkenazi: from Central and Eastern Europe
  • Sephardi: from Spain and Portugal, later North Africa, the Ottoman Empire, and elsewhere
  • Mizrahi: from the Middle East and North Africa
  • Ethiopian (Beta Israel), Yemenite, Indian, and other communities

Important Jewish Terminology

  • Torah: Teaching; the Five Books of Moses, or Jewish teaching in general
  • Tanakh: The Hebrew Bible: Torah, Prophets, and Writings
  • Talmud: Rabbinic discussions of law and lore
  • Mitzvah (plural mitzvot): Commandment; also a good deed
  • Halakha: Jewish law ("the way to walk")
  • Covenant (Brit): The relationship between God and Israel
  • Shabbat: The Sabbath, from Friday sunset to Saturday night
  • Synagogue: House of prayer, study, and assembly
  • Rabbi: Teacher and religious leader
  • Kosher: Fit, permitted according to Jewish dietary law
  • Tzedakah: Charity; literally "justice" or "righteousness"
  • Teshuvah: Repentance; returning
  • Tikkun olam: Repairing the world
  • Chesed: Loving-kindness
  • Bar / Bat Mitzvah: Coming of age as an adult in Jewish law
  • Shema: The central declaration of faith
  • Messiah (Mashiach): The anointed one who will bring redemption
  • Kippah: Head covering
  • Tallit: Prayer shawl
  • Tefillin: Leather boxes containing Torah verses, worn in prayer
  • Mezuzah: Scroll on the doorpost of a Jewish home

Basic Beliefs

Judaism is centered more on practice than on a formal creed, but the medieval philosopher Maimonides set out Thirteen Principles of Faith that are widely recited. They include belief in:

  1. God as the Creator
  2. God's unity
  3. God's non-physical nature
  4. God's eternity
  5. prayer to God alone
  6. prophecy
  7. the unique prophecy of Moses
  8. the divine origin of the Torah
  9. the unchanging nature of the Torah
  10. God's knowledge of human actions
  11. reward and punishment
  12. the coming of the Messiah
  13. the resurrection of the dead

The core relationship in Judaism is the covenant. Jewish tradition counts 613 commandments in the Torah: 248 positive commandments and 365 prohibitions.

Major practices include:

  1. prayer
  2. Torah study
  3. observing Shabbat and the festivals
  4. keeping kosher
  5. tzedakah (charity) and acts of kindness
  6. lifecycle rituals

Major Jewish Holidays

Rosh Hashanah

The Jewish New Year. It begins the High Holy Days, a time of reflection and repentance. The shofar (ram's horn) is blown.

Yom Kippur

The Day of Atonement, the holiest day of the Jewish year. It is a day of:

  • fasting
  • prayer
  • confession
  • seeking forgiveness
  • reconciliation

Passover (Pesach)

Celebrates the Exodus from slavery in Egypt. Families hold a Seder meal and retell the story using the Haggadah. Leavened bread is not eaten.

Shavuot

Commemorates the giving of the Torah at Mount Sinai.

Sukkot

The festival of booths. Families build and eat in temporary huts (sukkot), recalling the Israelites' journey in the wilderness.

Hanukkah

The festival of lights, remembering the rededication of the Temple in Jerusalem in the second century BCE. It is a minor festival in religious law, though it is widely celebrated.

Purim

Celebrates the rescue of the Jews of Persia, as told in the Book of Esther.

Shabbat

The weekly Sabbath is the most frequent and one of the most important Jewish holy days.

Core Beliefs

God

The central belief of Judaism is that God is one.

God is understood as:

  • Creator of the universe
  • eternal
  • without physical form
  • all-knowing and all-powerful
  • just and merciful
  • personally involved in history and human life

Judaism rejects:

  • polytheism
  • the worship of idols
  • any physical image of God
  • incarnation of God in human form
  • the worship of created beings

The Torah describes God as "compassionate and gracious, slow to anger, abounding in kindness and truth" (Exodus 34:6).

Patriarchs, Matriarchs, and Prophets

Judaism honors the ancestors of the Jewish people:

  • the patriarchs: Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob
  • the matriarchs: Sarah, Rebecca, Rachel, and Leah

It also honors the prophets, through whom God spoke, including:

  • Moses, the greatest prophet
  • Aaron and Miriam
  • Joshua
  • Samuel
  • Nathan
  • Elijah and Elisha
  • Isaiah
  • Jeremiah
  • Ezekiel
  • the twelve "minor" prophets, such as Amos, Hosea, and Micah
  • Deborah and Huldah, among the women prophets

Jewish tradition teaches that prophecy ended in the early Second Temple period.

Revelation

Judaism teaches that God revealed the Torah to Moses at Mount Sinai, in the presence of the whole people of Israel.

Traditional Judaism teaches that God gave two forms of Torah:

  • the Written Torah, the Five Books of Moses
  • the Oral Torah, an accompanying tradition of interpretation, later written down in the Mishnah and Talmud

Different Jewish movements understand revelation in different ways, from a literal giving of the text to an ongoing human response to the divine.

The Hebrew Bible (Tanakh)

The Hebrew Bible is the central scripture of Judaism. "Tanakh" is an acronym of its three parts:

  • Torah (Teaching): Genesis, Exodus, Leviticus, Numbers, Deuteronomy
  • Nevi'im (Prophets): including Joshua, Judges, Samuel, Kings, Isaiah, Jeremiah, Ezekiel, and the twelve minor prophets
  • Ketuvim (Writings): including Psalms, Proverbs, Job, Ruth, Esther, Song of Songs, and Ecclesiastes

It contains:

  • the story of creation
  • the history of Israel
  • laws and commandments
  • prophecy
  • poetry and prayer
  • wisdom literature
  • moral teachings

A handwritten Torah scroll (Sefer Torah) is the most sacred object in a synagogue. A portion is read each Shabbat, completing the whole Torah once a year.

The Talmud and Rabbinic Literature

The second major source for understanding Judaism is the Oral Torah, as recorded and developed by the rabbis.

Key texts include:

  • the Mishnah, compiled around 200 CE
  • the Jerusalem Talmud, about 400 CE
  • the Babylonian Talmud, about 500 CE, which is the most authoritative
  • the Midrash, interpretations and stories based on the Bible

The Talmud includes:

  • halakha: legal discussions
  • aggadah: stories, ethics, and theology

Later legal codes include Maimonides' Mishneh Torah and the Shulchan Aruch by Joseph Karo, with the glosses of Moses Isserles.

Angels and Spiritual Beings

The Hebrew Bible mentions angels (malakhim, "messengers") who carry out God's will.

Named angels include:

  • Michael
  • Gabriel
  • Raphael

In the Hebrew Bible, "the satan" ("the adversary") appears as an accuser, such as in the Book of Job. Judaism generally does not teach that Satan is an independent force of evil opposed to God.

Judaism also teaches that each person has a good inclination (yetzer hatov) and an evil inclination (yetzer hara).

Human Nature

Judaism teaches that every human being is created in the image of God (Genesis 1:27).

Human beings have dignity, free will, and moral responsibility.

Judaism does not teach that people inherit Adam's guilt. Each person is born pure, and each person is responsible for their own actions. A traditional morning prayer begins: "My God, the soul you have given me is pure."

Sin

Judaism describes sin (chet) as "missing the mark."

Jewish tradition distinguishes between:

  • sins against God
  • wrongs against other people

Yom Kippur atones for sins against God, but for wrongs against other people, one must first ask forgiveness from the person harmed (Mishnah Yoma 8:9).

Repentance

Teshuvah, "returning," is central to Judaism.

Maimonides describes repentance as:

  1. recognizing the wrongdoing
  2. stopping it
  3. sincerely regretting it
  4. confessing it before God
  5. resolving not to repeat it
  6. making amends and asking forgiveness from anyone harmed

The days from Rosh Hashanah to Yom Kippur, called the Ten Days of Repentance, are especially devoted to teshuvah.

Prayer and Worship

Jewish prayer is called tefillah.

Observant Jews pray three times a day:

  • Shacharit (morning)
  • Mincha (afternoon)
  • Maariv / Arvit (evening)

Prayer includes:

  • the Shema
  • the Amidah, a central standing prayer
  • blessings and psalms
  • reading from the Torah on certain days

Traditional communal prayer requires a minyan, a quorum of ten adults. Jews outside Israel traditionally face toward Jerusalem when praying.

Judaism links prayer with ethical action. The prophets taught that worship without justice is empty (Isaiah 1:11–17).

Charity

Tzedakah has a central place in Judaism. The word means "justice" or "righteousness," because giving to the poor is seen as a duty, not an option.

Two related concepts are:

Tzedakah

Giving to those in need, traditionally at least one-tenth of income where possible.

Gemilut chasadim

Acts of loving-kindness, such as visiting the sick, comforting mourners, and welcoming guests.

Maimonides described eight levels of charity. The highest is helping a person become self-sufficient, for example through a loan, partnership, or job.

Fasting

Judaism has several fast days. The most important is Yom Kippur, when adult Jews abstain from food and drink for about 25 hours.

Tisha B'Av, which mourns the destruction of the First and Second Temples and other tragedies, is also a full fast.

Fasting is linked to:

  • repentance
  • prayer
  • mourning
  • self-reflection

People who are ill, pregnant, or otherwise at risk are exempt. Jewish law requires them to eat if fasting would endanger their health.

Life

Judaism regards life as sacred and good.

The Torah commands: "Choose life" (Deuteronomy 30:19).

The principle of pikuach nefesh, saving a life, overrides almost all other commandments.

Human beings are expected to:

  • serve God
  • study Torah
  • observe the commandments
  • care for family
  • work
  • pursue justice
  • help others
  • repair the world

Judaism generally focuses more on how to live well in this world than on speculation about the next.

Death

Judaism treats death as a natural part of life and a time for honoring the dead and comforting mourners.

The Bible teaches that "the dust returns to the earth as it was, and the spirit returns to God who gave it" (Ecclesiastes 12:7).

Afterlife

Jewish beliefs about the afterlife are less detailed than in some other religions, and Jews hold a range of views.

Traditional Judaism teaches:

  1. the soul continues after death
  2. the World to Come (Olam HaBa)
  3. the coming of the Messiah
  4. the resurrection of the dead

The Mishnah teaches that "all Israel has a share in the World to Come," and later tradition taught that righteous people of all nations do as well.

Heaven

Gan Eden (the Garden of Eden) is a traditional term for the spiritual reward of the righteous after death.

Rabbinic tradition describes it as a place where the righteous enjoy closeness to God's presence.

Gehinnom

Gehinnom is a place of purification for souls after death. In most traditional teaching, it is temporary. A common tradition teaches that most souls spend no more than twelve months there.

Judgment

Judaism teaches that God judges every person with justice and mercy.

Rosh Hashanah is described as the Day of Judgment, when, according to tradition, each person's fate for the coming year is written, and on Yom Kippur it is sealed. Repentance, prayer, and charity are said to change a harsh decree.

Free Will and Providence

Judaism strongly affirms free will.

The Talmud teaches: "Everything is in the hands of Heaven except the fear of Heaven" (Berakhot 33b), meaning God decides much about our circumstances, but each person chooses whether to be good.

Rabbi Akiva taught: "Everything is foreseen, yet freedom of choice is given" (Pirkei Avot 3:15).

The mainstream Jewish position affirms both:

  • God's knowledge and providence
  • human freedom and responsibility

Morality

Jewish ethics are rooted in the Torah and developed by the prophets and rabbis.

Among the repeatedly emphasized virtues are:

  • justice
  • loving-kindness
  • truthfulness
  • humility
  • generosity
  • honoring parents
  • welcoming strangers
  • protecting the vulnerable
  • honest business
  • keeping promises
  • guarding one's speech

The Torah commands: "Love your neighbor as yourself" (Leviticus 19:18).

When asked to summarize the Torah, the sage Hillel said: "What is hateful to you, do not do to your neighbor. That is the whole Torah; the rest is commentary. Go and learn" (Talmud, Shabbat 31a).

The Ten Commandments (Exodus 20; Deuteronomy 5) are a foundation of Jewish ethics.

Family

Family is central to Jewish life. Many of the most important rituals, such as the Shabbat meal and the Passover Seder, take place at home.

Judaism places particular importance on:

  • parents
  • children
  • spouses
  • extended family
  • the elderly
  • orphans and widows

"Honor your father and your mother" is one of the Ten Commandments.

Marriage

Judaism considers marriage a sacred bond (kiddushin, "sanctification").

A traditional Jewish wedding includes:

  • the ketubah, a marriage contract that sets out the husband's obligations to his wife
  • the chuppah, a wedding canopy
  • the giving of a ring
  • the seven blessings (sheva brachot)
  • breaking a glass, recalling the destruction of the Temple

Jewish law allows divorce. In traditional law it requires a document called a get.

The movements differ on various marriage rules.

Community

Judaism places strong emphasis on community (kehillah).

The synagogue functions not only as a place of prayer but also as a center for:

  • study
  • education
  • celebrations
  • community meetings
  • charity
  • social support

Many communities also have a mikveh (ritual bath), schools, burial societies, and charitable organizations.

Treatment of Others

Jewish ethics apply to everyone.

The Torah repeatedly commands kindness to strangers:

"You shall love the stranger, for you were strangers in the land of Egypt." (Deuteronomy 10:19)

Rabbinic tradition teaches the Seven Laws of Noah, a universal moral code for all humanity. Non-Jews who keep these laws are considered righteous.

Judaism generally does not seek converts, though it accepts sincere converts.

Scriptures and Teachings

The Torah

The Torah is the foundational Jewish scripture.

Its major themes include:

  • God
  • creation
  • covenant
  • the patriarchs and matriarchs
  • slavery and Exodus
  • the giving of the law
  • worship
  • justice
  • holiness
  • the land of Israel

The Torah is simultaneously a theological, historical, moral, and legal text.

The Oral Torah and the Talmud

The Talmud answers practical questions such as:

  • How is Shabbat observed?
  • How do the dietary laws work?
  • How should business be conducted?
  • What are the duties within a family?
  • How should courts judge?
  • How should one pray?

The Talmud preserves disagreements as well as conclusions. Debate and questioning are part of Jewish learning.

Jewish Scholarship

Over the centuries, Jewish scholars developed learning in:

  • Bible commentary
  • Talmud study
  • Jewish law
  • philosophy
  • mysticism (Kabbalah)
  • ethics (Mussar)
  • Hebrew grammar
  • poetry

Major scholars include:

  • Hillel and Shammai
  • Rabbi Akiva
  • Rabbi Judah HaNasi, compiler of the Mishnah
  • Rashi
  • Maimonides (Rambam)
  • Nachmanides (Ramban)
  • Judah Halevi
  • Joseph Karo
  • Isaac Luria
  • the Vilna Gaon
  • the Baal Shem Tov

History

Origins

Jewish tradition traces its origins to Abraham, who left Mesopotamia in response to God's call, roughly 4,000 years ago according to traditional reckoning.

Abraham's grandson Jacob, also called Israel, had twelve sons, who became the twelve tribes of Israel.

Slavery and the Exodus

According to the Bible, the Israelites were enslaved in Egypt. God freed them through Moses, an event remembered each year at Passover.

At Mount Sinai, God gave the Torah and made a covenant with the people. After forty years in the wilderness, they entered the land of Canaan.

Kingdom and Temple

Around 1000 BCE, David established Jerusalem as the capital of a united kingdom. His son Solomon built the First Temple.

The kingdom later divided into Israel in the north and Judah in the south. The northern kingdom fell to Assyria in 722 BCE. The Babylonians destroyed the First Temple in 586 BCE and took many Jews into exile.

Jerusalem

Jerusalem is the holiest city in Judaism. The site of the Temple is the holiest place in Judaism.

The Western Wall, part of the retaining wall of the Second Temple complex, is today the most important place of Jewish prayer.

Jews pray facing Jerusalem, and many prayers express longing for it.

Second Temple and Diaspora

After the Persian king Cyrus allowed Jews to return, the Second Temple was built. The Romans destroyed it in 70 CE.

After this, rabbinic Judaism developed, focused on prayer, Torah study, and the synagogue. Most Jews lived in the diaspora for nearly 2,000 years, in the Middle East, North Africa, Europe, and beyond.

Major Historical Milestones

  • c. 2000–1700 BCE: Traditional period of Abraham.
  • c. 1300–1200 BCE: Traditional period of the Exodus and Sinai.
  • c. 1000 BCE: King David establishes Jerusalem as capital.
  • 586 BCE: Babylonians destroy the First Temple; Babylonian exile.
  • 516 BCE: Second Temple completed.
  • 164 BCE: Maccabean revolt; rededication of the Temple (Hanukkah).
  • 70 CE: Romans destroy the Second Temple.
  • c. 200 CE: Mishnah compiled.
  • c. 500 CE: Babylonian Talmud completed.
  • 10th–12th centuries: "Golden Age" of Jewish culture in Muslim Spain.
  • 1492: Jews expelled from Spain.
  • 18th century: Rise of Hasidism and the Jewish Enlightenment (Haskalah).
  • 19th century: Emergence of Reform, Conservative, and Orthodox movements; rise of Zionism.
  • 1881–1924: Mass migration of Eastern European Jews to the United States.
  • 1939–1945: The Holocaust: about six million Jews murdered by Nazi Germany and its collaborators.
  • 1948: Establishment of the State of Israel.
  • 21st century: Jewish life continues in diverse communities around the world.

Major Denominations and Movements

Orthodox Judaism

Modern Orthodox

Combines strict observance of Jewish law with engagement in modern society and education.

Haredi

Emphasizes separation from secular culture and intensive Torah study.

Hasidic

A movement founded in 18th-century Eastern Europe by the Baal Shem Tov, emphasizing joy, prayer, and devotion. Groups include Chabad-Lubavitch, Satmar, and Ger.

Conservative (Masorti) Judaism

Founded in 19th-century Germany and developed in the United States. It seeks to preserve Jewish law while allowing for historical development.

Reform Judaism

Founded in 19th-century Germany. It is the largest Jewish movement in the United States.

Reconstructionist Judaism

Founded in the 20th century by Mordecai Kaplan in the United States.

Other Groups

  • Karaite Judaism, which accepts only the written Bible as authoritative
  • Humanistic Judaism, a non-theistic movement
  • Jewish Renewal

These movements are all part of the Jewish people, though they disagree on important questions.

Traditional and Progressive Interpretations

Modern Jews differ significantly on:

  • the authority of Jewish law
  • the role of women
  • LGBTQ inclusion
  • conversion
  • intermarriage
  • Shabbat and dietary observance
  • the role of the State of Israel

Two Jews may share the same scripture and history while reaching very different conclusions.

Regional Differences

Israel

Jewish holidays are national holidays and Hebrew is the everyday language. Israelis range from very secular to very religious.

United States

American Jews include Reform, Conservative, Orthodox, Reconstructionist, and unaffiliated Jews, and also Israeli, Russian-speaking, Persian, Syrian, Ethiopian, and many other communities.

Europe

Jewish communities rebuilt after the Holocaust. France has the largest Jewish community in Europe.

Latin America

Significant communities in Argentina, Brazil, and Mexico.

Ethiopia, India, and elsewhere

Communities such as the Beta Israel of Ethiopia and the Bene Israel of India have their own long traditions.

Consequently, there is no single "Jewish culture."

Cultural Practices Versus Religious Requirements

It is important to distinguish between Jewish religious law and Jewish culture.

Examples of cultural practices that are not religious requirements include:

  • specific foods, such as bagels or gefilte fish
  • Yiddish or Ladino
  • klezmer music
  • particular wedding dances
  • regional clothing styles
  • certain family customs

A practice may be common among Jews without being religiously required. Conversely, the same religious law may be practiced differently in different communities.

Myths, Common Misconceptions, and Facts

Myth: Judaism is only a race or only a religion.
Fact: Judaism is both a religion and a people. People of every race and ethnicity are Jewish, and people can join through conversion.

Myth: Judaism is the religion of the "Old Testament" only.
Fact: Jews read the Hebrew Bible through the Oral Torah and the Talmud. Rabbinic Judaism has developed over 2,000 years.

Myth: Jews believe God is harsh and Christians believe God is loving.
Fact: The Hebrew Bible repeatedly describes God as merciful, compassionate, and loving. "Love your neighbor as yourself" comes from the Torah (Leviticus 19:18).

Myth: "Chosen people" means Jews think they are superior.
Fact: In Jewish teaching, being chosen means having been given responsibilities, not privileges. It is understood as a calling to live by the Torah.

Myth: Hanukkah is the "Jewish Christmas".
Fact: Hanukkah is a minor festival with its own history. The most important Jewish holy days are Shabbat, Rosh Hashanah, Yom Kippur, and the pilgrimage festivals.

Myth: Judaism has one uniform interpretation.
Fact: Jews hold a wide range of beliefs and practices. Debate is part of Jewish tradition; the Talmud preserves both majority and minority opinions.

Myth: All Jews are Israeli, and all Israelis are Jewish.
Fact: Most Jews outside Israel are citizens of other countries. About a quarter of Israel's citizens are not Jewish.

Religion and Everyday Life

Family

Family is central to Jewish life.

Jews are encouraged to maintain:

  • respect for parents
  • marriage
  • care for children
  • care for elderly relatives
  • family meals on Shabbat and holidays
  • relationships with neighbors

Marriage

Marriage is both a religious covenant and a legal agreement.

A Jewish marriage generally involves:

  • consent of both partners
  • a ketubah (marriage contract)
  • a ceremony under the chuppah
  • mutual responsibilities

Children

Children are regarded as a blessing and a responsibility.

Parents are expected to provide:

  • care
  • education, especially Torah education
  • protection
  • moral guidance
  • affection

Important rituals include brit milah (circumcision) for boys on the eighth day, naming ceremonies for girls, and Bar and Bat Mitzvah at 13 (12 for girls in many communities).

Education

Learning is one of Judaism's most important values. The Mishnah teaches that Torah study is equal to all the other commandments, because it leads to them.

Jewish communities developed:

  • schools for children
  • yeshivas for advanced study
  • study partnerships (chavruta)
  • libraries and publishing

Jewish communities historically placed high value on literacy.

Food

Jewish dietary law is called kashrut. Food that meets the rules is kosher.

Basic rules include:

  • only certain animals may be eaten (for example, no pork or shellfish)
  • meat and dairy may not be eaten together
  • animals must be slaughtered in a specific way (shechita)
  • blood may not be eaten

Observance varies widely, from strict to none.

Clothing

Judaism values modesty (tzniut).

Traditional items include:

  • kippah (head covering)
  • tallit (prayer shawl)
  • tzitzit (fringes)
  • tefillin, worn at weekday morning prayer
  • head coverings for many married Orthodox women

Clothing varies widely, from Hasidic dress to everyday modern clothing.

Gender Roles

Judaism teaches that men and women are both created in God's image.

Traditional Jewish law gives men and women different religious duties in some areas. Orthodox communities generally maintain these differences.

Reform, Conservative, and Reconstructionist Judaism ordain women as rabbis and count women in a minyan. Modern Jews differ substantially on these questions.

Work

Work is regarded positively in Judaism.

The Torah commands six days of work and one day of rest. The rabbis praised those who support themselves by their own work.

Jews are encouraged to:

  • work honestly
  • pay workers on time
  • avoid fraud
  • support their families
  • contribute to the community

Business Ethics

Jewish law has detailed rules for business, including:

  • honest weights and measures
  • no deceptive practices
  • paying workers promptly
  • not charging interest to fellow Jews (with legal mechanisms developed later)
  • returning lost property
  • fair competition

The Talmud teaches that one of the first questions a person is asked after death is: "Did you conduct your business honestly?"

Charity

Tzedakah is part of daily Jewish life.

Jews may give:

  • money to the poor
  • food
  • interest-free loans
  • support for education
  • community support
  • volunteer service

Many homes keep a charity box (pushke). Jewish communities have long supported free loan societies, burial societies, and care for the sick.

Medicine and Healthcare

Judaism regards saving life as one of the highest values. Most commandments may be set aside to save a life.

Jewish physicians made major contributions to medicine, including Maimonides, who was a physician as well as a rabbi.

Jewish law addresses issues such as:

  • end-of-life care
  • organ donation, widely supported to save a life
  • fertility treatment
  • autopsy
  • fasting while ill

Death and Funerals

Traditional Jewish funeral practice includes:

  • washing and preparing the body (tahara)
  • a simple shroud and plain coffin
  • burial, usually as soon as possible
  • the mourner's prayer, Kaddish
  • shiva, seven days of mourning at home
  • shloshim, thirty days of mourning
  • annual remembrance (yahrzeit)

Traditional Judaism forbids cremation, though some liberal Jews accept it.

Holidays

The most frequent holy day is Shabbat. The High Holy Days are Rosh Hashanah and Yom Kippur. The three pilgrimage festivals are Passover, Shavuot, and Sukkot.

Other observances include Hanukkah, Purim, Tisha B'Av, and Holocaust Remembrance Day (Yom HaShoah).

Community Life

Jewish community life commonly centers around:

  • synagogues
  • Jewish schools and day schools
  • community centers
  • charitable organizations
  • summer camps
  • youth movements
  • burial societies

In the United States, synagogues and Jewish community centers may also provide:

  • food assistance
  • social services
  • counseling
  • adult education
  • interfaith programs
  • refugee resettlement

Politics and Civic Participation

Jewish history includes many different political situations, from ancient kingdoms to minority communities across the world to the modern State of Israel.

A Talmudic principle, "the law of the land is the law" (dina d'malkhuta dina), teaches Jews to follow the civil law of the country they live in.

Jews today live in:

  • democracies
  • the State of Israel
  • countries where they are small minorities

In the United States, Jewish citizens participate in voting, public service, advocacy, charitable work, and interfaith work. American Jews were active in the civil rights movement.

Relationships With People of Other Faiths

Judaism has a long history of interaction with:

  • Christians
  • Muslims
  • Zoroastrians
  • Hindus
  • people of other religions
  • people without religious belief

Jews lived for centuries in Christian and Muslim societies, at times in periods of cooperation and at other times under persecution.

Since the 20th century, Jewish-Christian and Jewish-Muslim dialogue has grown. The Catholic Church's 1965 declaration Nostra Aetate was a turning point in Jewish-Catholic relations.

Judaism in America

Jews have lived in what is now the United States since 1654, when 23 Jews arrived in New Amsterdam.

American Jews include people with roots in:

  • Central and Eastern Europe
  • Spain, Portugal, and the Mediterranean
  • the Middle East and North Africa
  • Iran
  • Israel
  • the former Soviet Union
  • Ethiopia
  • Latin America

In 1790, George Washington wrote to the Jewish congregation in Newport, Rhode Island, that the government "gives to bigotry no sanction, to persecution no assistance."

American Jews today participate in every field, including medicine, law, science, business, education, government, journalism, and the arts.

Judaism and Humanity

Jewish civilization made major contributions in many areas.

Ethics and Law

The Hebrew Bible's teachings, including the Ten Commandments, the weekly day of rest, and care for the poor and stranger, have shaped Western law and ethics.

Medicine and Science

Jewish physicians and scientists have made major contributions across history, from Maimonides to modern science.

Literature and Thought

Jewish writers and thinkers have contributed to philosophy, literature, and the arts in many languages.

Judaism and Knowledge

Judaism encourages questioning and debate. The Talmud records arguments between schools and minority opinions.

Jewish tradition describes Torah study as lifelong. Pirkei Avot teaches: "Who is wise? One who learns from every person."

Judaism and Justice

Justice is one of the most important themes in Judaism.

The Torah commands:

"Justice, justice you shall pursue." (Deuteronomy 16:20)

The prophets repeatedly called for justice for the poor, the widow, the orphan, and the stranger.

Judaism and Human Dignity

Judaism teaches that every person is created in the image of God.

The Mishnah teaches that God created only one human being at first, so that no one could say "my ancestor is greater than yours," and that "whoever saves one life, it is as if they saved an entire world" (Mishnah Sanhedrin 4:5).

This provides a Jewish foundation for:

  • human equality
  • the value of each individual
  • opposition to racism
  • care for the stranger

Judaism and Repairing the World

Tikkun olam, "repairing the world," is a widely used idea in modern Judaism. It expresses the responsibility to make the world more just and whole.

Pirkei Avot teaches: "It is not your duty to finish the work, but neither are you free to abandon it" (Pirkei Avot 2:16).

Judaism as Torah, Worship, and Kindness

Judaism does not reduce religion to belief. A famous teaching in Pirkei Avot says:

"The world stands on three things: on Torah, on worship, and on acts of loving-kindness." (Pirkei Avot 1:2)

Torah

Study of God's teaching.

Avodah

Worship and service of God.

Gemilut Chasadim

Acts of loving-kindness.

This framework can be useful for understanding Judaism as learning + worship + kindness.

Conclusion

Judaism is a monotheistic tradition centered on the one God, the covenant with the people of Israel, the Torah, and the commandments.

Its central scripture is the Hebrew Bible, interpreted through the Oral Torah, the Talmud, and centuries of rabbinic tradition.

At its theological core are:

  • belief in one God
  • the covenant
  • revelation of the Torah
  • the prophets
  • reward and punishment
  • the hope for the Messiah and the World to Come

At its practical core are:

  • prayer
  • Torah study
  • Shabbat and the festivals
  • kashrut
  • tzedakah
  • lifecycle rituals

At its ethical core are:

  • justice
  • loving-kindness
  • honesty
  • care for the stranger
  • honoring parents
  • protecting life
  • repairing the world

Judaism has made deep contributions to religion, ethics, law, literature, and science. Jews today represent great diversity of language, country, observance, and culture.

Understanding Judaism requires distinguishing among scripture, law, belief, history, peoplehood, and contemporary practice.
