Jainism

An ancient Indian religion centered on the absolute practice of non-violence, asceticism, and the spiritual liberation of the soul.

Introduction

Jainism is an ancient religion of India. It teaches that every living being has a soul, and that the path to liberation is through non-violence, self-discipline, and spiritual knowledge.

Jains follow the teachings of the Tirthankaras, "ford-makers," who crossed the river of rebirth and showed others the way. The 24th and most recent Tirthankara was Mahavira, who lived in the 6th to 5th centuries BCE in what is now Bihar, India.

Jainism does not teach a creator God. Jains believe the universe is eternal and uncreated. They worship the Tirthankaras not as creators or as gods who grant favors, but as perfect souls who are models to follow.

Jainism is known above all for its commitment to ahimsa, non-violence toward every living being, from humans and animals to insects and plants.

A key Jain teaching summarizes this outlook:

"Souls render service to one another." (Parasparopagraho Jivanam — Tattvartha Sutra 5.21)

This phrase is the motto of the Jain community. It expresses the belief that all life is bound together in mutual support.

Jainism at a Glance

Name

Jainism, or Jain Dharma

A follower of Jainism is called a Jain.

The word Jain comes from jina, meaning "victor" or "conqueror." A jina is someone who has conquered inner passions such as anger, pride, deceit, and greed, and attained perfect knowledge.

Approximate Number of Adherents

India's 2011 census recorded about 4.5 million Jains, about 0.4% of India's population. Including the diaspora, there are an estimated 4 to 6 million Jains worldwide.

Global surveys usually include Jains in an "other religions" category, so precise figures vary.

Where Jainism Is Practiced

Most Jains live in India, especially in:

  • Maharashtra
  • Rajasthan
  • Gujarat
  • Madhya Pradesh
  • Karnataka

Diaspora communities are found in:

  • the United States and Canada
  • the United Kingdom
  • Kenya and East Africa
  • Belgium (especially the diamond trade in Antwerp)
  • Singapore and Malaysia
  • Japan
  • Australia

Jain sacred texts were composed in Ardhamagadhi Prakrit, Sanskrit, and other languages. Today Jains speak Gujarati, Hindi, Marathi, Kannada, and many other languages.

Major Branches and Traditions

The two main branches are:

Digambara

"Sky-clad." Digambara monks do not wear clothing as a sign of complete renunciation. Digambaras are strongest in Karnataka, Maharashtra, and Madhya Pradesh. They teach that women must be reborn as men before attaining liberation.

Shvetambara

"White-clad." Shvetambara monks and nuns wear white robes. Shvetambaras are strongest in Gujarat and Rajasthan. They teach that women can attain liberation.

Sub-groups include:

  • Shvetambara Murtipujaka (image-worshiping)
  • Sthanakvasi (non-image-worshiping)
  • Terapanthi (Shvetambara)
  • Digambara Bisapanthi and Terapanthi
  • modern movements such as those inspired by Shrimad Rajchandra and Kanji Swami

The branches agree on the core teachings while differing on monastic practice, scriptures, and certain beliefs.

Important Jain Terminology

  • Jina: Victor over inner passions
  • Tirthankara: "Ford-maker"; one of 24 great teachers
  • Jiva: Soul; living being
  • Ajiva: Non-living matter
  • Karma: Subtle matter that binds the soul
  • Ahimsa: Non-violence
  • Anekantavada: Many-sidedness; no single viewpoint holds the whole truth
  • Aparigraha: Non-possessiveness
  • Moksha: Liberation of the soul
  • Siddha: A liberated soul
  • Kevala jnana: Omniscience; perfect knowledge
  • Samsara: The cycle of birth and death
  • Three Jewels: Right faith, right knowledge, right conduct
  • Navkar Mantra: The most important Jain prayer
  • Derasar / Basadi: Jain temple
  • Sadhu / Sadhvi: Monk / nun
  • Shravaka / Shravika: Lay man / lay woman
  • Paryushana: The most important annual festival
  • Samayika: Meditation of equanimity
  • Pratikramana: Ritual of confession and reflection
  • Sallekhana: Voluntary fasting at the end of life

Basic Beliefs

Core Jain beliefs include:

  1. every living being has an eternal soul (jiva)
  2. the universe is eternal and uncreated
  3. the soul is bound by karma
  4. the soul goes through cycles of rebirth
  5. the soul can be freed through its own effort
  6. liberated souls (siddhas) dwell in eternal bliss and perfect knowledge

The path to liberation is the Three Jewels (Ratnatraya):

  1. Right faith (samyak darshana)
  2. Right knowledge (samyak jnana)
  3. Right conduct (samyak charitra)

The Tattvartha Sutra opens: "Right faith, right knowledge, and right conduct together are the path to liberation."

The five great vows are:

  1. Ahimsa — non-violence
  2. Satya — truthfulness
  3. Asteya — not stealing
  4. Brahmacharya — chastity
  5. Aparigraha — non-possessiveness

Monks and nuns follow these vows strictly. Lay Jains follow them in a more limited form.

Major Jain Holidays

Paryushana and Das Lakshana

The most important Jain festival, held in August or September. Shvetambaras observe eight days (Paryushana) and Digambaras ten days (Das Lakshana Parva).

It is a time of:

  • fasting
  • prayer and meditation
  • scripture reading
  • confession and reflection
  • asking forgiveness

It ends with Samvatsari, the day of forgiveness. Jains ask everyone they may have hurt for forgiveness, saying "Micchami Dukkadam," meaning "may my wrongdoing be forgiven."

Mahavir Jayanti

Celebrates the birth of Mahavira, usually in March or April.

Diwali

For Jains, Diwali marks Mahavira's attainment of liberation (moksha). Lamps are lit to symbolize the light of knowledge.

Other Observances

  • Akshaya Tritiya
  • Kartik Purnima
  • Mahamastakabhisheka, the ceremony anointing the statue of Bahubali at Shravanabelagola, held about every 12 years

Core Beliefs

The Universe and the Question of God

Jainism teaches that the universe was never created and will never end. It runs according to natural laws, including the law of karma.

Jainism does not teach a creator God who controls the universe or judges people. Every soul has the potential to become perfect, and liberated souls are honored as divine.

Jainism rejects:

  • a creator God
  • the idea that a god can remove a person's karma
  • animal sacrifice
  • violence of any kind
  • the idea that any single viewpoint holds the complete truth

The Tirthankaras

Jains believe that in each half-cycle of time, 24 Tirthankaras appear. They attain perfect knowledge and teach the path to liberation.

In the present age, the 24 Tirthankaras include:

  • Rishabhanatha (Adinatha), the first
  • Neminatha, the 22nd
  • Parshvanatha, the 23rd, who most historians agree was a historical person who lived around the 9th to 8th centuries BCE
  • Mahavira, the 24th

Jains do not consider Mahavira the founder of Jainism. They believe he revived and taught an eternal truth.

Revelation and Knowledge

In Jainism, truth is not revealed by God. It is known through perfect knowledge (kevala jnana), which the Tirthankaras attained.

The Tirthankaras' teachings were passed on by their chief disciples, called ganadharas, who arranged them into scriptures.

The Jain Scriptures (Agamas)

The Agamas are the main Jain scriptures.

Shvetambara canon

Shvetambaras accept a canon of about 45 texts (32 for Sthanakvasis and Terapanthis), including:

  • the Acharanga Sutra, on monastic conduct
  • the Sutrakritanga
  • the Kalpa Sutra, with the lives of the Tirthankaras
  • the Uttaradhyayana Sutra, on ethics and teaching

Digambara texts

Digambaras believe the original Agamas were lost. They rely on later texts, including:

  • the Shatkhandagama
  • the Kashayapahuda
  • the works of Kundakunda, such as the Samayasara

Shared text

The Tattvartha Sutra by Umaswati is accepted by both branches and is the best-known summary of Jain teaching.

These texts contain:

  • teachings on the soul and karma
  • ethics and vows
  • rules for monks and nuns
  • cosmology
  • the lives of the Tirthankaras
  • philosophy and logic

Spiritual Beings

Jain cosmology includes heavenly beings (devas), humans, animals and plants, and hell beings.

Gods are souls living in heavenly realms because of good karma. They are not eternal, and they cannot grant liberation.

Many Jain temples include guardian deities (yakshas and yakshis), such as Padmavati and Ambika, who protect the faith.

Human Nature

Jainism teaches that every soul is pure by nature, with infinite knowledge, perception, bliss, and energy.

This true nature is covered by karma, which builds up through actions driven by passions.

Human birth is especially valuable because only humans can practice the full path to liberation.

Jainism does not teach inherited sin. Each soul is responsible for its own karma.

Karma

Jainism has a distinctive understanding of karma. Karma is a kind of very fine matter that sticks to the soul when a person acts with passion.

The four main passions (kashayas) are:

  • anger
  • pride
  • deceit
  • greed

There are eight types of karma. Some block knowledge, perception, and energy; others determine a soul's body, lifespan, and experiences.

No god can remove karma. Each soul must free itself through its own effort.

Stopping and Removing Karma

Jainism teaches that karma can be:

  1. stopped from entering the soul (samvara) through self-control
  2. removed from the soul (nirjara) through austerity, meditation, and repentance

Daily and annual confession (pratikramana) helps Jains reflect on harm done and seek forgiveness. Jains ask forgiveness from all living beings with the words:

"I forgive all living beings. May all living beings forgive me. I have friendship with all, and enmity with none."

Prayer and Worship

The most important Jain prayer is the Navkar (Namokar) Mantra. It honors five kinds of holy beings:

  • Arihants (Tirthankaras and others with perfect knowledge)
  • Siddhas (liberated souls)
  • Acharyas (spiritual leaders)
  • Upadhyayas (teachers)
  • Sadhus and sadhvis (monks and nuns)

It names no individual, showing respect for qualities rather than persons.

Jain worship may include:

  • reciting the Navkar Mantra
  • temple worship (puja) with water, sandalwood, flowers, rice, and lamps
  • meditation (samayika)
  • reading scriptures
  • visiting monks and nuns
  • pilgrimage

Jains worship the Tirthankaras as ideals, not to ask for favors. Some Jain sects do not use images in worship.

Charity

Charity (dana) is an important duty for lay Jains.

Charity may involve:

  • giving food to monks and nuns
  • supporting temples and schools
  • building and running animal shelters (panjrapoles)
  • feeding the hungry
  • supporting hospitals
  • donating to education

Jain communities are known for their extensive charitable work.

Fasting

Fasting is one of the most important Jain practices. It is a form of austerity (tapas) that helps remove karma.

Common forms of fasting include:

  • complete fasting with only boiled water
  • eating one meal a day
  • eating only certain foods
  • not eating after sunset
  • fasting for many days during Paryushana

Fasting should be done with calm and equanimity, not as a display.

Life

Jainism regards all life as sacred.

Jains believe that even the smallest beings, such as insects and microorganisms, have souls. Living beings are classified by the number of senses they have, from one sense (plants, earth, water) to five senses (humans and animals).

Human beings are expected to:

  • avoid harming any living being
  • speak truthfully
  • live simply
  • control their passions
  • practice self-discipline
  • support the community
  • seek knowledge

Death

Death is the soul leaving its body. The soul then takes a new birth according to its karma.

Jains hope to die peacefully, with a calm mind, reciting the Navkar Mantra.

Rebirth

The soul is reborn in one of four states:

  • heavenly beings
  • humans
  • animals and plants
  • hell beings

Rebirth continues until the soul is freed from all karma.

Liberation (Moksha)

Moksha is the final goal of Jainism. When a soul is free from all karma, it rises to the top of the universe, to a place called Siddhashila.

There, the liberated soul (siddha) lives forever in:

  • infinite knowledge
  • infinite perception
  • infinite bliss
  • infinite energy

Heavens and Hells

Jain cosmology describes many heavens and hells. They are temporary. A soul stays there until the karma that caused that birth is used up. Neither is the final goal.

Judgment

Jainism has no judgment by God. The law of karma acts naturally and exactly. Each person experiences the results of their own actions.

Free Will

Jainism strongly emphasizes self-effort. The soul is responsible for its own bondage and its own liberation.

Morality

Jain ethics are based on the five vows.

Among the repeatedly emphasized virtues are:

  • non-violence
  • truthfulness
  • honesty
  • self-control
  • forgiveness
  • humility
  • simplicity
  • contentment
  • compassion
  • respect for all life

The Acharanga Sutra teaches:

"All beings are fond of life; they like pleasure and hate pain, shun destruction and like to live. To all, life is dear."

Many-Sidedness (Anekantavada)

Jainism teaches that reality is complex and can be seen from many viewpoints. No single viewpoint has the whole truth.

The famous story of the blind men and the elephant, each of whom touches a different part and describes it differently, is often used to explain this teaching.

Anekantavada encourages tolerance, humility, and respect for other views.

Family

Family is important in Jain life. Lay Jains are householders who support family, community, and monastics.

Jainism places particular importance on:

  • parents
  • children
  • spouses
  • elders
  • monks and nuns
  • the community

Marriage

Marriage is a social and religious institution in Jain life. Jain weddings include prayers, the Navkar Mantra, and vows. Customs vary by region and community.

Lay Jains are expected to be faithful in marriage under the vow of brahmacharya.

Community

The Jain community is called the fourfold sangha:

  • monks (sadhus)
  • nuns (sadhvis)
  • lay men (shravakas)
  • lay women (shravikas)

Temples and community halls serve as centers for worship, education, festivals, and charity.

Treatment of Others

Jain ethics apply to all living beings.

Jainism teaches non-violence in thought, word, and deed. It also teaches friendship toward all beings (maitri), joy in the virtues of others (pramoda), compassion for those who suffer (karunya), and tolerance toward those who disagree (madhyastha).

Scriptures and Teachings

The Agamas

The Agamas are the foundation of Jain scripture.

Their major themes include:

  • the soul
  • karma
  • non-violence
  • the vows
  • monastic discipline
  • the cosmos
  • the Tirthankaras
  • liberation

The Tattvartha Sutra

The Tattvartha Sutra, written around the 2nd to 5th centuries CE, sets out the whole Jain system in about 350 verses. It explains the seven basic truths (tattvas):

  1. soul (jiva)
  2. non-soul (ajiva)
  3. inflow of karma (asrava)
  4. bondage (bandha)
  5. stopping karma (samvara)
  6. removing karma (nirjara)
  7. liberation (moksha)

Jain Scholarship

Jain scholars made major contributions to:

  • logic and philosophy
  • mathematics
  • astronomy
  • grammar
  • literature in Prakrit, Sanskrit, Kannada, Tamil, and Gujarati
  • ethics

Major scholars include:

  • Kundakunda
  • Umaswati
  • Siddhasena Divakara
  • Samantabhadra
  • Haribhadra
  • Hemachandra
  • Yashovijaya

History

Origins

Jains believe their religion is eternal and has been taught by a series of Tirthankaras.

Historians generally agree that Parshvanatha, the 23rd Tirthankara, lived around the 9th to 8th centuries BCE, and Mahavira in the 6th to 5th centuries BCE.

Mahavira

Mahavira was born as Vardhamana into a royal family near present-day Patna, Bihar. Traditional Shvetambara accounts place his birth in 599 BCE; many scholars place him later, around the same period as the Buddha.

At about 30, he renounced his home and practiced intense austerity and meditation for about twelve and a half years. He then attained perfect knowledge (kevala jnana).

He taught for about 30 years and attained liberation at Pavapuri, Bihar.

His teaching emphasized:

  • non-violence
  • truthfulness
  • non-possessiveness
  • self-discipline
  • equality of all souls
  • liberation through one's own effort

Sacred Places

Important Jain pilgrimage sites include:

  • Shatrunjaya (Palitana), Gujarat, with over 800 temples
  • Mount Girnar, Gujarat
  • Sammed Shikhar, Jharkhand, where 20 Tirthankaras attained liberation
  • Pavapuri, Bihar, where Mahavira attained liberation
  • Shravanabelagola, Karnataka, with the giant statue of Bahubali
  • Dilwara Temples at Mount Abu, Rajasthan
  • Ranakpur Temple, Rajasthan

Expansion

Jainism spread across India with support from kings and merchants. According to tradition, the Mauryan emperor Chandragupta became a Jain monk and moved to South India.

Jainism was influential in Gujarat, Rajasthan, Karnataka, and Tamil Nadu. Jain merchants and scholars played major roles in Indian society.

In the 20th century, Jains migrated to East Africa, Britain, North America, and elsewhere.

Major Historical Milestones

  • c. 9th–8th century BCE: Life of Parshvanatha.
  • c. 6th–5th century BCE: Life of Mahavira.
  • c. 4th–3rd century BCE: Chandragupta Maurya and Bhadrabahu go to South India, according to tradition.
  • c. 1st century CE onward: Gradual division into Digambara and Shvetambara traditions.
  • 5th century CE: Council of Valabhi; Shvetambara scriptures are written down.
  • 981 CE: Statue of Bahubali completed at Shravanabelagola.
  • 11th–13th centuries: Dilwara temples built; the scholar Hemachandra flourishes.
  • 15th–18th centuries: Rise of the Sthanakvasi and Terapanthi movements.
  • 1893: Virchand Gandhi represents Jainism at the World's Parliament of Religions in Chicago.
  • 20th century: Mahatma Gandhi, influenced by Jain teachings, promotes non-violence; Jain diaspora communities form.
  • 2014: The Indian government recognizes Jains as a national minority community.

Major Historical Figures

Important figures include:

  • Rishabhanatha
  • Parshvanatha
  • Mahavira
  • Gautama Swami (Indrabhuti), Mahavira's chief disciple
  • Chandana, head of Mahavira's community of nuns
  • Bhadrabahu
  • Kundakunda
  • Umaswati
  • Hemachandra
  • Shrimad Rajchandra, spiritual guide to Mahatma Gandhi
  • Virchand Gandhi
  • Acharya Tulsi
  • many monks, nuns, scholars, merchants, and patrons across Jain history

Major Sects and Traditions

Digambara

Monks practice complete renunciation, including of clothing. They eat once a day from their hands.

Shvetambara

Monks and nuns wear white. The Shvetambara community includes:

  • Murtipujaka, who worship in temples with images
  • Sthanakvasi, who do not use images and meet in halls (sthanaks)
  • Terapanthi, a reform tradition founded in the 18th century

Modern Movements

  • followers of Shrimad Rajchandra
  • followers of Kanji Swami
  • Jain organizations in the diaspora that bring sects together

These traditions are all regarded as Jain rather than as separate religions.

Traditional and Modern Interpretations

Modern Jains differ on:

  • the role of temples and images
  • women's spiritual status
  • strictness of diet, including dairy
  • use of technology by monks
  • adapting to life abroad
  • environmental activism

Two Jains may share the same core beliefs while reaching different conclusions on modern questions.

Regional Differences

Gujarat and Rajasthan

Mostly Shvetambara, with large temple complexes.

Karnataka and Maharashtra

Large Digambara communities.

Diaspora

Communities in the US, UK, and Kenya often build temples shared by different sects.

United States

American Jains include immigrants from India and East Africa, their children, and some converts. The Federation of Jain Associations in North America (JAINA) connects communities.

Cultural Practices Versus Religious Requirements

It is important to distinguish between Jain teaching and regional culture.

Examples of cultural practices that are not core Jain requirements include:

  • particular wedding customs
  • regional dress
  • certain foods unrelated to non-violence
  • caste-like community divisions
  • business traditions

Myths, Common Misconceptions, and Facts

Myth: Jainism is a sect of Hinduism or Buddhism.
Fact: Jainism is an independent religion with its own scriptures, teachers, and philosophy.

Myth: Jains worship Mahavira as God.
Fact: Jains honor Mahavira and the other Tirthankaras as perfect souls and models, not as a creator God who grants favors.

Myth: Jainism is only for monks.
Fact: Most Jains are lay people who follow the vows in a form suited to family and working life.

Myth: Non-violence means Jains are passive.
Fact: Non-violence in Jainism is an active discipline of care for all life, expressed in charity, animal welfare, and peaceful action.

Myth: The Jain swastika is related to the Nazi symbol.
Fact: The swastika is an ancient Jain symbol of the four states of existence and of well-being. Its meaning was distorted by the Nazis.

Myth: All Jains wear masks.
Fact: Only some monks and nuns, mainly in the Sthanakvasi and Terapanthi traditions, wear a mouth cloth (muhapatti) to avoid harming tiny beings.

Myth: Jainism has one uniform practice.
Fact: Digambara and Shvetambara traditions, and their sub-groups, differ significantly.

Religion and Everyday Life

Family

Family is central to Jain life.

Jains are encouraged to maintain:

  • respect for parents and elders
  • faithful marriage
  • care for children
  • care for elderly relatives
  • hospitality to monks and nuns

Marriage

Marriage is a social and religious bond.

A Jain wedding generally involves:

  • prayers and the Navkar Mantra
  • vows of mutual responsibility
  • family blessings

Children

Children are taught Jain values at home, at temple classes (pathshala), and through stories.

Parents are expected to provide:

  • care
  • education
  • protection
  • moral guidance
  • affection

Education

Learning is highly valued. Jains preserved large manuscript libraries, called gyan bhandars, some of which are among the oldest libraries in India.

Jain communities today support schools, colleges, and scholarships.

Food

Jain food practice is guided by non-violence.

Common practices include:

  • strict vegetarianism
  • avoiding root vegetables such as potatoes, onions, garlic, and carrots, because harvesting them kills the whole plant and many small organisms
  • not eating after sunset (chauvihar)
  • avoiding honey and alcohol
  • filtering water
  • many Jains are now vegan, avoiding dairy because of harm to animals

Dietary practice varies among individuals and sects.

Clothing

Jainism encourages simplicity.

Shvetambara monks and nuns wear white. Digambara monks wear no clothing. Lay Jains dress according to local culture, often wearing simple clothes for temple worship.

Gender Roles

Women and men are both lay members and monastics in Jainism. Jain nuns outnumber monks.

The two branches differ on whether a woman can attain liberation in this life: Shvetambaras say she can, and Digambaras say she must first be reborn as a man.

Modern Jains discuss women's roles in leadership and worship.

Work

Jains are encouraged to choose work that causes the least possible harm.

Many Jains have traditionally worked in trade, banking, jewelry, and later in medicine, law, technology, and business. Farming was often avoided because it harms small creatures in the soil.

Business Ethics

Jain business ethics emphasize:

  • honesty
  • not cheating
  • limiting possessions and greed
  • giving to charity
  • avoiding businesses that harm animals

The vow of aparigraha encourages Jains to limit what they own and to give surplus wealth away.

Charity

Charity is integrated into Jain life.

Jains may give:

  • food and shelter
  • support to animal shelters and bird hospitals
  • support for schools and hospitals
  • disaster relief
  • support for monks and nuns

Medicine and Healthcare

Jainism values the protection of life. Jains generally accept modern medicine, though some avoid medicines made from animal products.

Jains support many hospitals, including animal and bird hospitals such as the Charity Birds Hospital in Delhi.

Death and Funerals

Jain funeral practice generally includes:

  • reciting the Navkar Mantra
  • cremation as soon as possible
  • a time of prayer and reflection

Sallekhana is an ancient Jain practice of gradually reducing food at the very end of life, when death is near, with permission of a spiritual teacher. Jains see it as a peaceful and conscious acceptance of death, not suicide. Its legal status has been debated in India.

Holidays

The major Jain observances are Paryushana and Das Lakshana, Samvatsari, Mahavir Jayanti, and Diwali.

Community Life

Jain community life commonly centers around:

  • temples
  • upashrayas (lodgings for monks and nuns)
  • pathshalas (religious schools)
  • charitable trusts
  • festivals

In the United States, Jain centers may also provide youth programs, language classes, meditation, and interfaith programs.

Politics and Civic Participation

There is no single Jain political system. Jains live as minorities in India and abroad and participate in civic life.

Jain principles of non-violence influenced Mahatma Gandhi and, through him, the civil rights movement.

Relationships With People of Other Faiths

Jainism has a long history of interaction with:

  • Hindus
  • Buddhists
  • Muslims
  • Sikhs
  • Christians
  • Zoroastrians

Anekantavada encourages Jains to respect other views as partial truths.

Jainism in America

Jains began arriving in the United States in significant numbers after 1965. JAINA was founded in 1981. American Jains have built temples and centers across the country and are active in medicine, technology, business, and science.

Jainism and Humanity

Jain civilization made major contributions in many areas.

Non-Violence

Jain teaching on ahimsa influenced Gandhi and modern movements for non-violence, vegetarianism, and animal welfare.

Art and Architecture

Jain temples such as Dilwara and Ranakpur are masterpieces of marble carving.

Mathematics and Science

Jain texts discuss very large numbers, types of infinity, and the classification of living beings.

Libraries

Jain manuscript libraries preserved many ancient Indian texts.

Jainism and Knowledge

Right knowledge is one of the Three Jewels. Jain logic developed sophisticated ways of describing truth from multiple perspectives, such as syadvada ("maybe" or conditional statements).

Jainism and Justice

Jain teaching connects justice with non-violence and honest dealing. Hurting others, including by exploitation, binds the soul with karma.

Jainism and Human Dignity

Jainism teaches that every soul is equal in its potential for perfect knowledge and bliss. This includes animals and other beings.

This provides a Jain foundation for:

  • equality
  • respect for all life
  • environmental care
  • tolerance

Jainism and the Environment

Jain teachings of non-violence, non-possessiveness, and the interdependence of all life are often linked to environmental protection. Living simply and limiting consumption are part of Jain practice.

Jainism as Faith, Knowledge, and Conduct

Jainism does not reduce religion to belief. The Three Jewels present the path as:

Right Faith

Trust in the truth taught by the Tirthankaras.

Right Knowledge

Accurate understanding of the soul, karma, and reality.

Right Conduct

Living by the vows, especially non-violence.

This framework can be useful for understanding Jainism as faith + knowledge + conduct.

Current Challenges

The primary challenge for modern Jains is maintaining the strict ethical and dietary requirements of Ahimsa in a rapidly globalized, fast-paced world where hidden animal products and exploitative supply chains are ubiquitous.

For the diaspora, passing down the incredibly complex philosophical concepts and the strict lifestyle requirements to youth growing up in Western, consumer-driven cultures presents an ongoing existential challenge to the community's traditional way of life.

Conclusion

Jainism is an ancient Indian tradition centered on the eternal soul, karma, non-violence, and liberation through one's own effort.

At its core are:

  • the Three Jewels
  • the five vows
  • the teaching of the Tirthankaras
  • karma and rebirth
  • the goal of moksha

At its practical core are:

  • prayer and meditation
  • fasting
  • careful diet
  • confession and forgiveness
  • charity

At its ethical core are:

  • non-violence
  • truthfulness
  • non-possessiveness
  • forgiveness
  • many-sidedness and tolerance
  • respect for all life

Though small in number, Jains have had a large influence on Indian culture, business, art, and the global movement for non-violence.

Understanding Jainism requires distinguishing among scripture, philosophy, monastic practice, lay practice, and culture.
