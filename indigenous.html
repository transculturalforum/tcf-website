Indigenous Traditions

A vast, diverse array of earth-centered spiritualities practiced by the original inhabitants of lands across the globe, deeply rooted in kinship with nature and ancestors.

Introduction

"Indigenous spiritual traditions" is an umbrella term for the religious and spiritual ways of life of the world's Indigenous peoples. These include Native American and First Nations peoples of North America, Indigenous peoples of Latin America, Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples of Australia, the Māori of Aotearoa New Zealand, the Sámi of northern Europe, Native Hawaiians, and many peoples of Africa, Asia, the Arctic, and the Pacific.

These traditions are not one religion. Each people has its own language, ceremonies, sacred stories, and relationship with its land. What they often share is a worldview in which spiritual life cannot be separated from land, community, ancestors, and everyday life.

Many Indigenous peoples do not have a separate word for "religion." Spirituality is understood as a way of living in right relationship with the Creator or spiritual powers, with other people, with animals and plants, and with the land.

The Lakota phrase "Mitákuye Oyás'iŋ," often translated "all my relatives" or "we are all related," expresses a theme found in many Indigenous traditions:

All beings are connected, and each has a responsibility to the others.

Many Indigenous people today practice traditional spirituality, Christianity, Islam, or another religion, or combine traditional ways with another faith.

Indigenous Traditions at a Glance

Name

There is no single name. Each people uses its own terms for its spiritual ways, often meaning simply "our way," "the law," or "the teachings."

Commonly used English terms include:

  • Indigenous spirituality
  • traditional religion
  • Native spirituality
  • African traditional religions
  • Aboriginal spirituality

The terms "pagan," "primitive," and "animist" have often been used in ways that are disrespectful and are best avoided unless a community uses them itself.

Approximate Number of People

The United Nations estimates that there are more than 476 million Indigenous people living in 90 countries, about 6% of the world's population. They represent around 5,000 different cultures.

Not all Indigenous people practice traditional spirituality. Many are Christian, Muslim, Buddhist, or of other faiths, and many combine traditions. Surveys that count "folk" or "traditional" religions use different definitions, so numbers vary widely.

Where Indigenous Traditions Are Practiced

Indigenous peoples live on every inhabited continent, including:

  • North America (Native American, First Nations, Inuit, and Métis peoples)
  • Central and South America (including Maya, Quechua, Aymara, Mapuche, and Amazonian peoples)
  • Australia (Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples)
  • the Pacific (Māori, Native Hawaiians, and many Pacific Islander peoples)
  • Africa (including the Yoruba, Akan, Zulu, San, Maasai, and many others)
  • the Arctic and northern Europe (including the Sámi and Inuit)
  • Asia (including the Ainu of Japan, Adivasi peoples of India, and many peoples of Southeast Asia and Siberia)

Indigenous peoples speak thousands of languages. Language is often closely connected to spiritual knowledge, because ceremonies, prayers, and sacred names are carried in the language.

Major Families of Tradition

Because each people is distinct, it is more accurate to speak of regional families of tradition than of branches. Examples include:

Native American and First Nations Traditions

Hundreds of nations, each with their own ceremonies, such as the Sun Dance, the sweat lodge, the potlatch, and the Longhouse traditions of the Haudenosaunee.

Aboriginal Australian Traditions

Centered on the Dreaming, the ancestral law and creation stories connected to specific country (land).

African Traditional Religions

Including Yoruba (Ifá and Òrìṣà), Akan, Igbo, Zulu, and many others, often with a supreme Creator, spirits, and ancestors.

Māori and Polynesian Traditions

Centered on whakapapa (genealogy), mana (spiritual authority), and tapu (sacredness).

Sámi and Arctic Traditions

Including the noaidi (spiritual specialist), the drum, and sacred places in the landscape.

Latin American Indigenous Traditions

Including Andean reverence for Pachamama (Mother Earth) and the apus (mountain spirits), and Maya calendar and ceremonial traditions.

African Diaspora Religions

Some traditions with African roots developed in the Americas, such as Vodou, Santería (Lucumí), and Candomblé. They are often discussed alongside Indigenous traditions, though they have their own histories.

Important Terminology

Each people has its own terms. The following are general English concepts and a few widely known examples, used with care:

  • Creator / Great Spirit: A supreme or originating power recognized by many peoples
  • Ancestors: Those who came before, who may continue to guide the living
  • Elders: Respected keepers of knowledge and teachings
  • Oral tradition: Knowledge passed on through speech, song, and story
  • Ceremony: A sacred event that renews relationships
  • Sacred site: A place with spiritual significance
  • Medicine: Spiritual power, healing, or a sacred object or plant
  • Reciprocity: Giving back in return for what is received
  • Relationality: The understanding that all beings are related
  • Totem / clan: A relationship between a family group and an animal or other being
  • Healer / spiritual leader: A person with special knowledge for healing and ceremony
  • The Dreaming: Aboriginal Australian term for ancestral law and creation
  • Whakapapa: Māori term for genealogy that links people to the land and the gods
  • Pachamama: Andean term for Mother Earth
  • Òrìṣà: Yoruba divine beings
  • Smudging: Purifying with the smoke of sacred plants (in some traditions)

Common Themes

While beliefs differ greatly, scholars and Indigenous elders often identify these shared themes:

  1. a Creator or spiritual powers present in the world
  2. the relationship of all beings
  3. the sacredness of land
  4. respect for ancestors
  5. reciprocity and balance
  6. the passing on of knowledge through oral tradition and ceremony

Many traditions also emphasize:

  • gratitude
  • respect
  • humility
  • generosity
  • responsibility to future generations

The Haudenosaunee (Iroquois) principle of considering the impact of decisions on the seventh generation is one well-known example.

Major Ceremonies and Observances

There are no worldwide Indigenous holidays. Ceremonies follow each people's calendar, often tied to seasons, harvests, the movement of the sun, moon, and stars, and stages of life.

Seasonal and Harvest Ceremonies

Examples include the Green Corn Ceremony of several Eastern Woodlands nations, first-salmon ceremonies of Pacific Northwest peoples, and the Andean Inti Raymi (Festival of the Sun).

New Year Observances

Examples include Matariki, the Māori New Year, marked by the rising of the Pleiades star cluster, which became a public holiday in New Zealand in 2022.

Gatherings

Powwows in North America are social and cultural gatherings with dance, song, and food. Some parts are sacred.

Remembrance Days

Many countries now observe days such as the International Day of the World's Indigenous Peoples (August 9), Indigenous Peoples' Day in the United States, and NAIDOC Week in Australia.

Core Beliefs

The Creator and Spiritual Powers

Many Indigenous traditions recognize a Creator or supreme power, sometimes described as distant and sometimes as present in all things.

Examples include:

  • Wakan Tanka, "the Great Mystery," among the Lakota
  • Olódùmarè, the supreme being of the Yoruba
  • Nyame, the sky god of the Akan
  • Io, the supreme being in some Māori traditions

Many traditions also recognize other spiritual beings, such as spirits of the land, water, and sky, culture heroes, and ancestors.

Indigenous traditions generally reject:

  • the idea that humans are separate from nature
  • the idea that land is only property
  • the idea that the spiritual world is separate from everyday life

Teachers, Ancestors, and Culture Heroes

Most Indigenous traditions do not have prophets in the Abrahamic sense. Instead, they honor:

  • ancestors who founded the people
  • culture heroes who brought teachings, such as the White Buffalo Calf Woman in Lakota tradition, who brought the sacred pipe
  • ancestral beings who shaped the land, such as the Rainbow Serpent in many Aboriginal traditions
  • trickster figures such as Coyote or Raven, whose stories teach lessons through humor
  • prophets in some traditions, such as the Haudenosaunee prophet Handsome Lake

Revelation and Sacred Knowledge

Sacred knowledge often comes through:

  • stories handed down from the ancestors
  • dreams and visions
  • ceremony
  • observation of the land and animals
  • guidance from elders

In some traditions, people seek a vision through fasting and prayer, for example in a vision quest.

Sacred Knowledge and Oral Tradition

Most Indigenous traditions have no written scripture. Sacred knowledge is kept in:

  • creation stories
  • songs
  • dances
  • ceremonies
  • art and designs
  • place names
  • wampum belts and other recording systems
  • the land itself, which holds memory

Some knowledge is open to everyone. Other knowledge is restricted to people who have been initiated or who hold certain responsibilities. Respecting these limits is important.

Some written texts exist, such as the Popol Vuh, a K'iche' Maya sacred narrative written down in the 16th century.

Spiritual Beings

Many traditions describe a world full of spiritual life. This may include:

  • spirits of animals, plants, rivers, and mountains
  • ancestor spirits
  • guardian spirits
  • beings who live in the sky, water, or under the earth

Scholars often use the word "animism" for the belief that the natural world is alive with spirit. Many Indigenous people describe it instead as recognizing that all beings are relatives.

Human Nature

Many Indigenous traditions teach that humans are one part of the web of life, not its masters.

Humans are often described as the "youngest" or most dependent of beings, who must learn from animals, plants, and the land.

Each person is born with gifts and responsibilities, and identity is closely connected to family, clan, and land.

Wrongdoing and Imbalance

In many traditions, wrongdoing is understood as breaking relationship or upsetting balance:

  • disrespecting the land or animals
  • breaking sacred law
  • harming others in the community
  • failing to give back

Such actions are believed to harm not only the person who acts but also the family, community, and land.

Restoring Balance

Many Indigenous traditions emphasize restoring relationship rather than only punishing.

This may involve:

  1. acknowledging the harm
  2. making offerings or prayers
  3. apologizing to those harmed
  4. ceremonies of cleansing or healing
  5. making amends to the family and community

Restorative practices such as talking circles and peacemaking courts, used in some Native American and First Nations communities, have influenced modern restorative justice.

Prayer, Ceremony, and Worship

Prayer and ceremony are central to many Indigenous ways of life.

Examples include:

  • offerings of tobacco, food, or other gifts
  • smudging with sacred plants such as sage, sweetgrass, or cedar
  • sweat lodge ceremonies
  • song and drumming
  • dance
  • pipe ceremonies
  • giving thanks at the start of gatherings
  • ceremonies at sacred sites

The Haudenosaunee Thanksgiving Address, spoken at the opening of gatherings, gives thanks to the people, the earth, the waters, the plants, the animals, the winds, the sun, the moon, the stars, and the Creator.

Generosity and Sharing

Generosity is highly valued in many Indigenous cultures.

Examples include:

  • the potlatch of Pacific Northwest peoples, where hosts give away gifts
  • giveaways at powwows and ceremonies
  • sharing the harvest or hunt with elders and families in need
  • feeding guests

A person's status is often measured by what they give, not what they keep.

Fasting

Fasting is part of some ceremonies, such as vision quests and the Sun Dance, where participants may go without food or water for a time as an act of sacrifice and prayer.

These practices are guided by elders and spiritual leaders.

Life

Many Indigenous traditions regard life as a sacred gift with responsibilities.

People are expected to:

  • respect elders
  • care for children
  • care for the land
  • share with others
  • keep the ceremonies
  • learn the language and teachings
  • live in balance

Many traditions mark stages of life, such as birth, naming, coming of age, marriage, and becoming an elder, with ceremonies.

Death

Death is often seen as a journey to the spirit world or a return to the ancestors.

Funerals and mourning practices are important for helping the spirit on its journey and supporting the family.

Afterlife and the Ancestors

Beliefs about the afterlife vary greatly. Many traditions teach that:

  • the dead join the ancestors
  • ancestors remain connected to the living
  • ancestors can guide and protect the living
  • the living have duties to remember and honor the dead

Some traditions include beliefs in reincarnation within the family or community. Others describe a spirit world or land of the dead.

Judgment and Accountability

Most Indigenous traditions do not focus on a single final judgment. Accountability is often understood through relationships: actions have consequences for the community, the land, and future generations.

Fate and Responsibility

Many traditions teach that people have gifts and paths given by the Creator or ancestors, but that each person is responsible for how they live.

Morality

Indigenous ethics often center on respect and responsibility.

Values frequently taught include:

  • respect
  • humility
  • honesty
  • generosity
  • courage
  • wisdom
  • love
  • reciprocity
  • care for the land
  • respect for elders

The Seven Grandfather Teachings of the Anishinaabe are one well-known example: wisdom, love, respect, bravery, honesty, humility, and truth.

Family and Kinship

Kinship is central in Indigenous cultures. It often extends beyond the immediate family to:

  • clans
  • extended families
  • adopted relatives
  • animals and natural beings considered relatives

Kinship systems often guide marriage, responsibilities, and ceremony.

Marriage

Marriage customs vary greatly. They often involve the families and wider community, and may include gift exchanges and ceremonies.

Community

In many Indigenous traditions, the community, rather than the individual, is the center of spiritual life.

Gathering places may include:

  • longhouses
  • ceremonial grounds
  • kivas
  • marae (Māori meeting grounds)
  • community halls

Treatment of Others

Many Indigenous traditions emphasize hospitality and respect toward visitors.

They also teach respect toward animals, plants, and the land as fellow beings.

Sacred Knowledge and Teachings

Creation Stories

Creation stories explain how the world, the people, and the land came to be, and teach how people should live.

Examples include:

  • the Haudenosaunee story of Sky Woman, who fell to earth and was helped by the animals to create land on the back of a turtle
  • the Aboriginal Australian Dreaming stories
  • the Māori story of Ranginui (Sky Father) and Papatūānuku (Earth Mother)
  • the Yoruba story of Ọbàtálá creating the land

The Role of Elders

Elders are honored as keepers of knowledge. They pass on language, stories, ceremonies, and law, and guide decision-making.

Indigenous Knowledge Systems

Indigenous peoples developed sophisticated knowledge in:

  • astronomy
  • ecology and land management
  • medicine and healing plants
  • agriculture, such as the "Three Sisters" of corn, beans, and squash
  • navigation, such as Polynesian wayfinding across the Pacific
  • governance

This knowledge is increasingly recognized in science and environmental management.

History

Origins

Indigenous spiritual traditions are among the oldest in the world. Aboriginal Australian cultures, for example, are among the oldest continuous cultures on earth, with a history in Australia of at least 50,000 to 65,000 years.

Each tradition developed over thousands of years in close relationship with a particular place.

Colonization

From the 15th century onward, European colonization had devastating effects on Indigenous peoples, including:

  • loss of land
  • disease
  • violence and war
  • forced removal
  • bans on ceremonies and languages
  • forced conversion

In the United States and Canada, governments outlawed many ceremonies. Canada banned the potlatch from 1885 to 1951. U.S. regulations restricted the Sun Dance and other ceremonies from the 1880s.

Residential and Boarding Schools

In the United States, Canada, Australia, and elsewhere, Indigenous children were taken from their families and sent to schools designed to erase their languages and cultures.

Canada's Truth and Reconciliation Commission (2015) described this as cultural genocide. Australia's "Stolen Generations" refers to children removed from their families.

Revival and Recognition

In the 20th and 21st centuries, many Indigenous peoples have worked to revive their languages, ceremonies, and traditions.

Major Historical Milestones

  • Tens of thousands of years ago: Development of Indigenous cultures on every continent.
  • 1492 onward: European colonization of the Americas.
  • 1788: British colonization of Australia begins.
  • 1840: Treaty of Waitangi signed between the British Crown and Māori chiefs.
  • 1880s–1930s: Bans on Indigenous ceremonies in the United States and Canada.
  • 1978: The American Indian Religious Freedom Act in the United States.
  • 1990: The Native American Graves Protection and Repatriation Act (NAGPRA).
  • 1994: The American Indian Religious Freedom Act is amended to protect the use of peyote in Native American Church ceremonies.
  • 2007: The United Nations adopts the Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples.
  • 2008: Australia's national apology to the Stolen Generations.
  • 2015: Canada's Truth and Reconciliation Commission final report.
  • 21st century: Language revival, land rights, and cultural renewal continue.

Major Historical Figures

Important figures include:

  • Hiawatha and the Peacemaker, founders of the Haudenosaunee Confederacy
  • Handsome Lake, Seneca prophet
  • Tecumseh and Tenskwatawa
  • Sitting Bull
  • Black Elk, Lakota holy man
  • Wovoka, leader of the Ghost Dance movement
  • Te Whiti o Rongomai, Māori leader of non-violent resistance
  • Vine Deloria Jr., author and scholar
  • Rigoberta Menchú, K'iche' Maya activist and Nobel laureate
  • many elders, healers, leaders, and knowledge keepers

Diversity of Traditions

Distinct Peoples

Each Indigenous people has its own spiritual tradition. It is important to name the specific people and not generalize.

Traditional and Blended Practice

Many Indigenous people combine traditional ways with Christianity or other religions. Examples include:

  • the Native American Church, which combines traditional and Christian elements
  • Andean Catholic festivals that include reverence for Pachamama
  • African Independent Churches that blend Christianity with African traditions

Some people practice only traditional ways. Others are Christian or follow another faith while keeping their culture.

Revival Movements

Many communities are reviving ceremonies and languages that were suppressed.

Regional Differences

North America

Hundreds of distinct nations, many with treaty relationships with governments.

Latin America

Indigenous traditions often blend with Catholicism.

Africa

Traditional religions remain important and often exist alongside Christianity and Islam.

Australia and the Pacific

The Dreaming, ancestral law, and connection to country are central for Aboriginal peoples; whakapapa and the marae for Māori.

United States

There are 574 federally recognized tribes, along with state-recognized and unrecognized tribes, each with its own traditions.

Culture, Spirituality, and Commercialization

It is important to distinguish authentic Indigenous practice from commercial imitations. Many Indigenous communities object to:

  • selling ceremonies
  • "plastic shamans," who claim false Indigenous spiritual authority
  • use of sacred items as fashion
  • sports mascots based on Indigenous peoples

Myths, Common Misconceptions, and Facts

Myth: All Indigenous peoples have the same religion.
Fact: There are thousands of distinct Indigenous peoples, each with its own traditions.

Myth: Indigenous traditions are a thing of the past.
Fact: Indigenous traditions are living practices, carried on and revived by communities today.

Myth: Indigenous peoples worship nature.
Fact: Many Indigenous traditions honor the land and its beings as relatives and as sacred. This is better described as respect and relationship than as "nature worship."

Myth: Oral tradition is less reliable than written history.
Fact: Oral traditions have preserved detailed knowledge for thousands of years. Some Aboriginal Australian stories are believed to describe sea-level changes from more than 7,000 years ago.

Myth: Anyone can practice Indigenous ceremonies.
Fact: Many ceremonies belong to specific peoples and require proper teaching and permission.

Myth: Indigenous people cannot also be Christian or Muslim.
Fact: Many Indigenous people practice other religions, sometimes alongside their traditions.

Religion and Everyday Life

Family

Family and kinship are central.

Indigenous peoples are often encouraged to maintain:

  • respect for elders
  • care for children by the whole community
  • strong extended family ties
  • clan responsibilities
  • connection to ancestors

Marriage

Marriage customs vary and often involve the whole family and community.

Children

Children are often seen as sacred gifts. Raising children is shared by parents, grandparents, aunts, uncles, and the wider community.

Naming ceremonies and coming-of-age ceremonies are important in many traditions.

Education

Traditional education happens through:

  • storytelling
  • observation
  • apprenticeship
  • ceremony
  • time on the land

Many communities today run language-immersion schools, such as Māori kōhanga reo.

Food

Food is often sacred. Traditional foods such as wild rice, salmon, corn, bison, and seal are central to many cultures.

Practices include:

  • giving thanks before eating
  • offering the first portion to the spirits or ancestors
  • sharing the harvest or hunt
  • taking only what is needed

Clothing

Traditional dress and regalia are used in ceremonies and gatherings. Some items, such as eagle feathers, are sacred and earned.

In daily life, most Indigenous people wear everyday modern clothing.

Gender Roles

Gender roles vary widely. Many traditions give women important spiritual and political roles. For example, Haudenosaunee clan mothers choose chiefs.

Some North American Indigenous cultures recognize people with more than two gender roles. The term "Two-Spirit" was adopted in 1990 as a modern umbrella term.

Work

Traditional work, such as hunting, fishing, gathering, and farming, is often spiritual as well as practical.

Economic Ethics

Many Indigenous traditions emphasize:

  • sharing
  • taking only what is needed
  • sustainable use of resources
  • responsibility to future generations

Charity and Sharing

Sharing is a duty in many cultures. Giveaways, feasts, and caring for those in need are common.

Medicine and Healing

Many traditions have healers who use plants, prayer, and ceremony.

Many Indigenous people today use both traditional healing and modern medicine. Some hospitals now include Indigenous healing programs.

Death and Funerals

Funeral practices vary greatly. They may include:

  • wakes and gatherings
  • songs and prayers
  • feasts
  • giveaways
  • mourning periods
  • ceremonies to help the spirit's journey

Many communities have worked to bring back ancestral remains held in museums, so that they can be properly reburied.

Holidays and Ceremonies

Ceremonies follow each people's seasonal calendar. Public observances include Indigenous Peoples' Day, Matariki, and National Day for Truth and Reconciliation in Canada.

Community Life

Community life commonly centers around:

  • ceremonies
  • powwows and gatherings
  • community councils
  • language programs
  • cultural centers
  • youth programs

Politics and Civic Participation

Many Indigenous nations have their own governments, laws, and treaties. In the United States, federally recognized tribes are sovereign nations.

Indigenous people participate in national politics, land rights movements, and environmental protection.

Relationships With People of Other Faiths

Indigenous peoples have long histories of interaction with Christian, Muslim, and other missionaries and neighbors, ranging from cooperation to forced conversion.

Many churches have since apologized for their role in residential schools and colonization. Indigenous leaders are active in interfaith dialogue, especially on care for the earth.

Indigenous Traditions in America

Native Americans have lived in the Americas for many thousands of years.

For much of U.S. history, Native ceremonies were restricted or banned. The American Indian Religious Freedom Act of 1978 recognized the right of Native Americans to practice their religions.

Today Native Americans continue to protect sacred sites, revive languages, and practice ceremonies, while participating in every part of American life.

Indigenous Traditions and Humanity

Ecology

Indigenous land management, such as cultural burning in Australia and North America, is increasingly recognized for environmental value.

Democracy

Historians debate the extent to which the Haudenosaunee Confederacy influenced American democratic ideas. In 1988 the U.S. Congress passed a resolution acknowledging that influence.

Agriculture

Indigenous peoples of the Americas developed corn, potatoes, tomatoes, beans, and many other foods now eaten worldwide.

Navigation

Polynesian navigators crossed thousands of miles of ocean using stars, waves, and birds.

Indigenous Traditions and Knowledge

Indigenous knowledge is based on long observation of the natural world. It is increasingly valued alongside scientific knowledge.

Indigenous Traditions and Justice

Many Indigenous approaches to justice focus on healing relationships and restoring balance.

Indigenous Traditions and Human Dignity

Many traditions teach that every person, and every being, has a place and a purpose in creation.

The UN Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples (2007) affirms Indigenous peoples' right to practice their spiritual traditions and protect their sacred sites.

Indigenous Traditions and the Earth

Care for the land is central to many Indigenous traditions. Indigenous peoples manage or hold rights to a large share of the world's land, which includes much of the world's remaining biodiversity.

Indigenous Traditions as Relationship, Ceremony, and Responsibility

Indigenous traditions often present spirituality as:

Relationship

With the Creator, ancestors, people, animals, plants, and land.

Ceremony

Renewing these relationships through prayer, song, and ritual.

Responsibility

Living in a way that honors those relationships and protects future generations.

This framework can be useful for understanding Indigenous traditions as relationship + ceremony + responsibility.

Conclusion

Indigenous spiritual traditions are among the oldest and most diverse in the world. They are not one religion but many living traditions.

Common themes include:

  • a Creator or spiritual powers
  • the relationship of all beings
  • the sacredness of land
  • respect for ancestors and elders
  • reciprocity and balance
  • oral tradition

At their practical core are:

  • ceremony
  • prayer and gratitude
  • sharing
  • passing on language and knowledge
  • care for the land

At their ethical core are:

  • respect
  • humility
  • generosity
  • responsibility to future generations
  • care for all relatives

Despite colonization and suppression, Indigenous peoples continue to keep and revive their traditions.

Understanding Indigenous traditions requires listening to Indigenous peoples themselves and recognizing the distinct traditions of each people.
