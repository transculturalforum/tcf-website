Secular Worldviews

A broad category encompassing atheism, agnosticism, and humanism, relying on reason, science, and human empathy rather than supernatural beliefs to understand the universe and guide morality.

Introduction

"Secular worldviews" is an umbrella term for ways of understanding life without relying on religious belief. It includes atheism, agnosticism, secular humanism, freethought, rationalism, and the outlook of many people who describe themselves as having "no religion."

Secular worldviews are not a religion. They have no shared scripture, no founder, and no required beliefs. What they often share is the view that human beings can understand the world through reason, evidence, and experience, and can live meaningful, ethical lives without belief in God or the supernatural.

The word "secular" comes from the Latin saeculum, meaning "this age" or "this world." It refers to what belongs to ordinary human life rather than to religion.

Secular humanism, the most developed of these outlooks, holds that people have the right and responsibility to give meaning and shape to their own lives. Humanists International summarizes humanism as:

"A democratic and ethical life stance which affirms that human beings have the right and responsibility to give meaning and shape to their own lives." (Humanists International, Minimum Statement on Humanism)

Many secular people value human rights, democracy, science, free inquiry, and the separation of religion and government.

Secular Worldviews at a Glance

Name

Common terms include:

  • Atheist: a person who does not believe in God or gods
  • Agnostic: a person who believes that the existence of God is unknown or unknowable
  • Humanist / secular humanist: a person who holds a non-religious ethical life stance centered on human welfare
  • Freethinker: a person who forms opinions through reason rather than authority or tradition
  • Skeptic: a person who questions claims that lack evidence
  • "Nones" / religiously unaffiliated: people who do not identify with any religion

These categories overlap. Many people who say they have "no religion" still believe in God or a higher power, or describe themselves as "spiritual but not religious."

Approximate Number of People

According to Pew Research Center, there were approximately 1.9 billion religiously unaffiliated people worldwide in 2020, about 24.2% of the world's population. This group grew by about 270 million between 2010 and 2020, more than any group except Muslims.

"Religiously unaffiliated" includes atheists, agnostics, and people who describe their religion as "nothing in particular." Not all of them hold a secular worldview in the philosophical sense.

Where Secular Worldviews Are Common

Religiously unaffiliated people live in every region of the world.

Large unaffiliated populations or high proportions are found in:

  • China
  • Japan
  • South Korea
  • Vietnam
  • the Czech Republic
  • the Netherlands
  • the United Kingdom
  • Scandinavia
  • Australia and New Zealand
  • Canada
  • the United States
  • Uruguay

China has the largest number of religiously unaffiliated people in the world. In the United States, about 28% of adults were religiously unaffiliated in 2023, according to Pew.

Major Streams and Traditions

Atheism

The absence of belief in God or gods. Some atheists actively deny that God exists, and others simply lack belief.

Agnosticism

The view that the existence of God cannot be known. The term was coined by Thomas Henry Huxley in 1869.

Secular Humanism

An ethical, non-religious life stance based on reason, compassion, and human welfare.

Freethought and Rationalism

Movements that emphasize reason and evidence over authority and tradition.

Religious Humanism and Cultural Religion

Some people hold humanist values while taking part in religious communities or traditions for cultural reasons, such as Humanistic Judaism or cultural Christians.

Secularism

A political principle that religion and government should be separate. Secularism is held by many religious people as well as non-religious people.

Important Terminology

  • Secular: Not connected with religion
  • Secularism: Separation of religion and state
  • Atheism: Absence of belief in God or gods
  • Agnosticism: The view that God's existence is unknown or unknowable
  • Humanism: An ethical life stance focused on human welfare and reason
  • Naturalism: The view that only the natural world exists
  • Empiricism: Knowledge comes from experience and evidence
  • Rationalism: Reason as the chief source of knowledge
  • Scientific method: Testing ideas through observation and experiment
  • Skepticism: Questioning claims and asking for evidence
  • Freethought: Forming beliefs by reason, not authority
  • Ethics: Principles of right and wrong
  • Utilitarianism: Ethics based on the greatest good for the greatest number
  • Human rights: Rights belonging to all people
  • Celebrant: A person who leads non-religious ceremonies
  • "Nones": People with no religious affiliation

Basic Principles

Secular worldviews have no creed, but many secular humanists share the following principles:

  1. naturalism: the natural world is all that exists
  2. reason and evidence as the best guides to truth
  3. the scientific method as the most reliable way to understand the world
  4. ethics based on human welfare, empathy, and reason
  5. human dignity and human rights
  6. democracy and freedom of thought
  7. separation of religion and government

The Amsterdam Declaration of 2002, adopted by Humanists International, lists core humanist values, including:

  1. humanism is ethical
  2. humanism is rational
  3. humanism supports democracy and human rights
  4. humanism insists that personal liberty must be combined with social responsibility
  5. humanism is a response to the demand for an alternative to dogmatic religion
  6. humanism values artistic creativity and imagination
  7. humanism is a life stance aiming at the maximum possible fulfillment

Notable Observances

Secular worldviews have no religious holidays. Some secular and humanist days include:

Darwin Day (February 12)

Celebrates science and the birthday of Charles Darwin.

World Humanist Day (June 21)

Celebrates humanist values around the June solstice.

International Day of Reason / National Day of Reason

Observed by some groups in the United States.

Human Rights Day (December 10)

Marks the adoption of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights.

Many secular people also celebrate cultural holidays, such as Christmas, Lunar New Year, Diwali, or Thanksgiving, as family and cultural events.

Core Ideas

The Question of God

Secular worldviews do not include belief in God as part of their outlook.

  • Atheists do not believe in God or gods, usually because they find the evidence or arguments unconvincing.
  • Agnostics believe that the question cannot be answered with certainty.
  • Some secular people do not consider the question important.

Secular humanists generally reject:

  • belief in the supernatural without evidence
  • the idea that morality depends on religion
  • the use of religion to justify discrimination
  • religious authority over government

At the same time, secular humanism supports freedom of religion and belief for everyone, including religious people.

Thinkers and Teachers

Secular worldviews have no prophets. They draw on a long line of thinkers, scientists, and reformers from many cultures, including:

  • the Charvaka school of ancient India, which rejected the supernatural
  • Confucius, who focused on ethics and human relationships
  • Epicurus and Democritus in ancient Greece
  • Lucretius in ancient Rome
  • Enlightenment philosophers such as David Hume, Voltaire, and Denis Diderot
  • Charles Darwin, Bertrand Russell, and many modern scientists and philosophers

Sources of Knowledge

Secular worldviews do not accept revelation as a source of knowledge. Instead they rely on:

  • observation
  • reason
  • the scientific method
  • critical thinking
  • the shared experience of humanity

Knowledge is understood as open to revision when new evidence appears.

Key Texts and Thinkers

Secular worldviews have no scripture. Important texts include:

  • Lucretius, On the Nature of Things
  • David Hume, Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion
  • Thomas Paine, The Age of Reason
  • John Stuart Mill, On Liberty and Utilitarianism
  • Charles Darwin, On the Origin of Species
  • Bertrand Russell, Why I Am Not a Christian
  • the Humanist Manifestos (1933, 1973, 2003)
  • the Amsterdam Declaration (2002)
  • Carl Sagan, The Demon-Haunted World

These texts are respected but are not treated as sacred or authoritative.

Spiritual Beings

Most secular people do not believe in spirits, angels, demons, or other supernatural beings. They explain experiences often described as supernatural through psychology, neuroscience, or natural causes.

Human Nature

Secular humanists generally view human beings as part of nature, shaped by evolution.

Humans are seen as:

  • capable of reason
  • social beings
  • capable of empathy and cooperation
  • capable of both good and harm
  • responsible for their own choices

Secular humanism does not include the idea of sin against God. People are understood to have both strengths and flaws that can be improved through education, community, and good institutions.

Wrongdoing

In secular ethics, wrongdoing is usually understood as causing harm, treating people unfairly, breaking trust, or violating rights.

Making Amends

Secular ethics emphasize:

  1. recognizing harm
  2. taking responsibility
  3. apologizing
  4. repairing harm
  5. learning and changing

Reflection and Ritual

Secular people do not pray to God, but many practice:

  • meditation and mindfulness
  • reflection and journaling
  • appreciation of art, music, and nature
  • gratitude practices
  • community gatherings

Some humanist communities hold regular meetings with talks, music, and discussion.

Charity

Many secular people believe that helping others is a moral duty. Examples include:

  • donating to effective charities
  • volunteering
  • disaster relief
  • supporting education and healthcare
  • blood and organ donation

The effective altruism movement, which uses evidence to find the most effective ways to help others, includes many secular people.

Fasting

Secular worldviews have no religious fasting. Some secular people fast for health reasons or join religious friends in solidarity.

Life

Secular humanists generally believe that this life is the only one, which makes it precious.

People are encouraged to:

  • live meaningfully
  • seek knowledge
  • love and care for others
  • contribute to society
  • enjoy life responsibly
  • reduce suffering
  • protect the planet for future generations

Meaning is not given from outside but created through relationships, work, creativity, and service.

Death

Most secular people view death as the natural and permanent end of the individual's life. Many find comfort in the legacy they leave, in the memory of loved ones, and in being part of nature.

Epicurus wrote: "Death is nothing to us; for when we exist, death is not present, and when death is present, we do not exist."

Afterlife

Most secular people do not believe in an afterlife. Some agnostics remain open to the question.

Accountability

Secular ethics hold that people are accountable to one another, to society, and to their own conscience, rather than to divine judgment.

Free Will

Secular thinkers hold a range of views on free will, from belief in genuine free choice to the view that choices are determined by brain processes. Most agree that people should be held responsible for their actions in practical life.

Morality

Secular ethics draw on reason, empathy, and human experience.

Major approaches include:

  • consequentialism, which judges actions by their results
  • virtue ethics, which focuses on character
  • deontology, which focuses on duties and rights
  • the "Golden Rule," found in many cultures

Values frequently emphasized include:

  • honesty
  • fairness
  • compassion
  • responsibility
  • respect for others
  • freedom
  • equality
  • curiosity
  • courage
  • tolerance

Family

Family is important to most secular people, and many hold family values similar to their neighbors of any faith.

Marriage

Marriage is seen as a civil and personal commitment. Many secular couples have civil weddings or humanist ceremonies led by trained celebrants.

Community

Secular people find community in families, friendships, workplaces, clubs, and civic organizations.

Organized secular communities include:

  • humanist associations
  • Ethical Culture societies
  • freethought groups
  • Sunday Assembly congregations
  • student secular groups

Treatment of Others

Secular humanism emphasizes the equal dignity of all people. The Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948) is often cited by humanists as a statement of shared values.

Key Texts and Ideas

The Humanist Manifestos

The Humanist Manifesto I (1933) was written in the United States. It was followed by Humanist Manifesto II (1973) and Humanism and Its Aspirations (2003).

Major themes include:

  • reason and science
  • ethics without religion
  • democracy
  • human rights
  • the welfare of all people

The Enlightenment

The Enlightenment of the 17th and 18th centuries promoted reason, science, individual rights, and religious tolerance. Its ideas shaped modern democracy and the separation of church and state.

Secular Scholarship

Secular thinkers have contributed to:

  • philosophy
  • science
  • political theory
  • ethics
  • psychology
  • literature

History

Origins

Doubt about religious claims is very old. Examples include:

  • the Charvaka (Lokayata) school in ancient India
  • skeptical passages in the Rig Veda's Hymn of Creation
  • Greek philosophers such as Democritus, Epicurus, and Protagoras
  • Lucretius in Rome
  • Confucian focus on ethics over the supernatural

The Enlightenment

In 17th- and 18th-century Europe, thinkers emphasized reason and evidence. Some, such as Baron d'Holbach, openly argued for atheism. Others, such as Thomas Jefferson and Thomas Paine, were deists.

The Rise of Secularism

In the 19th century, the term "secularism" was coined by George Jacob Holyoake in Britain. Freethought movements grew in Europe and America.

The Twentieth Century and Today

The 20th century saw both the growth of humanism and the rise of states that enforced atheism, such as the Soviet Union, which persecuted religious believers. Secular humanists distinguish their support for freedom of belief from state-imposed atheism.

In recent decades, the number of religiously unaffiliated people has grown in many countries.

Major Historical Milestones

  • c. 6th century BCE onward: Charvaka materialism in India; early Greek naturalism.
  • 1st century BCE: Lucretius writes On the Nature of Things.
  • 17th–18th centuries: The Enlightenment.
  • 1791: The First Amendment of the U.S. Constitution forbids establishment of religion.
  • 1859: Darwin publishes On the Origin of Species.
  • 1869: Huxley coins the term "agnostic."
  • 1876: Felix Adler founds the Ethical Culture movement in New York.
  • 1933: Humanist Manifesto I.
  • 1948: Universal Declaration of Human Rights.
  • 1952: International Humanist and Ethical Union (now Humanists International) founded.
  • 2002: The Amsterdam Declaration.
  • 21st century: Rapid growth of the religiously unaffiliated in many countries.

Major Movements and Viewpoints

Secular Humanism

Organized humanism, represented by groups such as Humanists International, Humanists UK, and the American Humanist Association.

Atheist Organizations

Groups that advocate for the rights of non-believers and for separation of church and state.

Rationalist Movements

In India, rationalist movements challenge superstition and caste.

"Spiritual but Not Religious"

Many people without religious affiliation have spiritual beliefs or practices but reject organized religion.

Different Approaches

Secular people differ on:

  • how critical to be of religion
  • whether to form communities and rituals
  • the role of religion in public life
  • free will
  • political issues

Two secular people may share the same rejection of religious belief while disagreeing about almost everything else.

Regional Differences

Europe

Many countries have large secular populations and long histories of humanist organizations.

East Asia

Many people do not identify with a religion but take part in traditional practices such as ancestor rituals.

India

Rationalist movements have challenged superstition and caste.

United States

American "nones" include atheists, agnostics, and many who believe in God but do not belong to a religion.

Cultural Practices Versus Secular Beliefs

Many secular people continue cultural traditions linked to religion, such as holiday meals, music, and family customs, without religious belief.

Myths, Common Misconceptions, and Facts

Myth: Secular people have no morals.
Fact: Secular ethics are based on empathy, reason, and concern for others. Research has not shown that non-religious people are less moral than religious people.

Myth: Atheism is a religion.
Fact: Atheism is the absence of belief in God. It has no scripture, rituals, or required beliefs.

Myth: Secularism means opposition to religion.
Fact: Secularism means that government does not favor any religion. Many religious people support secularism because it protects their freedom.

Myth: Secular people have no meaning in life.
Fact: Many secular people find deep meaning in relationships, work, creativity, service, and the natural world.

Myth: All "nones" are atheists.
Fact: Many religiously unaffiliated people believe in God or a higher power.

Myth: Science and religion must conflict.
Fact: Many secular people and religious people value science. Secular people simply do not include the supernatural in their worldview.

Worldview and Everyday Life

Family

Secular people value family relationships and often raise children to think critically and act kindly.

Marriage

Marriage may be a civil ceremony or a humanist ceremony. In some countries, such as Scotland, humanist weddings are legally recognized and very popular.

Children

Parents are expected to provide:

  • care
  • education
  • protection
  • moral guidance
  • affection

Humanist naming ceremonies are an alternative to religious ceremonies for welcoming a child.

Education

Secular humanists place strong emphasis on education, science literacy, and critical thinking.

Food

Secular worldviews have no dietary rules. Many secular people choose vegetarian or other diets for ethical, environmental, or health reasons.

Clothing

There is no secular dress code.

Gender Roles

Secular humanism generally supports full equality between women and men and LGBTQ rights.

Work

Work is valued as a way to support oneself and contribute to society.

Business Ethics

Secular business ethics emphasize honesty, fairness, transparency, and social responsibility.

Charity

Many secular people give to charity and volunteer. Secular charities and humanist groups run aid programs worldwide.

Medicine and Healthcare

Secular people generally rely on evidence-based medicine. Most support organ donation, and many support medical research.

Secular views on end-of-life questions, such as assisted dying, vary, though many humanist organizations support the right to choose.

Death and Funerals

Secular funerals and memorials often include:

  • celebrating the person's life
  • stories and tributes
  • music and readings
  • burial or cremation
  • leadership by a family member or humanist celebrant

Holidays

Secular people often celebrate cultural and national holidays. Some observe humanist days such as Darwin Day and World Humanist Day.

Community Life

Secular community life commonly centers around:

  • humanist and freethought groups
  • volunteering
  • discussion groups
  • science and education events
  • online communities

Politics and Civic Participation

Secular people participate in all areas of political life. Many advocate for:

  • separation of religion and state
  • freedom of thought and expression
  • human rights
  • science-based policy

In some countries, atheists face legal discrimination or punishment. Humanists International publishes the Freedom of Thought Report on this issue.

Relationships With People of Other Faiths

Many secular people work alongside religious people in interfaith and community organizations, on shared goals such as human rights, poverty, and the environment.

Secular Worldviews in America

The U.S. Constitution establishes no official religion and forbids religious tests for public office.

The share of Americans with no religious affiliation grew from under 10% in the 1990s to around 28% in 2023, according to Pew.

Secular Americans participate in every field, including science, medicine, education, business, government, and the arts.

Secular Worldviews and Humanity

Science

Scientific inquiry, which secular thinkers champion, has transformed medicine, technology, and understanding of the universe.

Human Rights

Enlightenment thinkers, both religious and secular, helped develop the ideas behind modern human rights.

Democracy

The separation of religion and state, supported by both religious and secular people, protects freedom of belief for all.

Secular Worldviews and Knowledge

Secular worldviews value questioning, evidence, and the willingness to change one's mind.

Secular Worldviews and Justice

Many secular ethics focus on fairness, equality before the law, and reducing suffering.

Secular Worldviews and Human Dignity

Secular humanism holds that every person has equal worth because of their shared humanity.

Secular Worldviews and Compassion

Empathy is central to secular ethics. Many secular people see compassion as a natural human capacity to be developed.

Secular Humanism as Reason, Ethics, and Human Flourishing

Secular humanism can be understood through three dimensions:

Reason

Using evidence and critical thinking to understand the world.

Ethics

Acting with compassion and responsibility.

Human Flourishing

Working for a world where all people can live full and free lives.

This framework can be useful for understanding secular humanism as reason + ethics + flourishing.

Conclusion

Secular worldviews are ways of understanding life without religious belief, including atheism, agnosticism, and secular humanism.

At their core are:

  • naturalism
  • reason and evidence
  • science
  • ethics based on human welfare
  • freedom of thought

At their practical core are:

  • education
  • civic participation
  • community and service
  • non-religious ceremonies

At their ethical core are:

  • honesty
  • fairness
  • compassion
  • human rights
  • equality
  • responsibility

The religiously unaffiliated make up about a quarter of the world's population and represent a great variety of views.

Understanding secular worldviews requires distinguishing among atheism, agnosticism, humanism, secularism, and the many people who are simply unaffiliated.
